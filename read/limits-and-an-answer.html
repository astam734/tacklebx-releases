In brief The evidence behind this series is thinnest in three places: what a superintelligent machine would want and keep wanting as it changes itself, how much any proposed measure lowers the risk, and what happens once a machine can beat every human check. What survives is conditional. In the war models, a machine that acts as one, chooses rationally and seeks power and a longer life is bleak news if its arrival brings a large, fast jump in power and nothing holds it to its promises; shareable wants with shareable inputs leave more room for a deal, and a security problem can still return from the human side even when the machine can credibly promise to stop. Four candidate policies could make sense whatever one believes about the risk, but only if a condition that is assumed for two beliefs, and not shown, held for every pivotal party’s estimate of the risk. A pivotal party is a state or a regulated lab whose participation matters. Ordered on my judgment by how plainly each would pass that test, not by any effect on the risk at arrival, governing AI’s misuse comes first, and no effect on arrival is claimed for it. The models cannot tell a student who asked whether AI will end humanity what to do, and my own advice to her, which is not a finding, is that absent clear evidence that the world is going to end she should carry on as before while staying informed.

Part 1. The series ends with a list of where its evidence is thin, and an answer to one student

An argument built on analogy owes its reader two things at the end. One is an honest list of where its analogies are thinnest. The other is an answer to the question it began with. That question came in Introduction, the opening paper of this series of mine on the move to superintelligent AI. There a worried student asked whether artificial intelligence will eliminate humanity, and whether she should change course.

To answer her, I ask six questions:

  • where the evidence behind the argument is weakest;
  • how much of the verdict survives those gaps;
  • whether anyone can say, with a defensible number, how much proposed measures lower the risk;
  • which policy choices make sense without that number;
  • what the series proposes;
  • what the student should do.

The short answers come first. The evidence is thinnest on what wants a superintelligence would hold and keep as it modifies itself. It is also thin on how large the risk reductions from policy and technical measures are, and on whether those measures fail independently. And it is thin on what happens if a machine comes to dominate every human-side check. What survives is a conditional logic about promises at a machine’s arrival, and the series does not settle whether its “if” parts hold.

Four of five candidate policies would pass a test of making sense across risk estimates, but only if an unproved condition that is assumed for just two beliefs held for every pivotal party’s belief (Part 9). On my own judgment, the need to study what training builds into a machine also survives. The series proposes many conditional measures, each with the standing its own paper gives it, and ranks none across the series (Part 10). The models cannot answer the student, so my advice to her is personal counsel, not a finding (Part 11).

Several judgments here are mine rather than findings of the series. I flag each where it comes up:

  • that Gartzke’s conditional even-odds ceiling on predicting war when private information (facts one side knows and the other does not) drives conflict caps how well a model can fit the data, not whether the theory can be tested (Part 5);
  • that a machine’s control over its own agenda and inputs can speed a shift in power, whereas recognition and standing need not, if their conversion into power is blocked (Part 5);
  • that no other human-side protection can be relied on once a machine can defeat every human-side check (Part 6);
  • the order in which I place the candidate policies, and my verdict that keeping the most powerful systems in view does not pass while a dispute about the race stays open (Part 9);
  • that research on what training builds in is the most direct priority bearing on whether a machine would value us (Part 9);
  • that a caution drawn from one experiment extends to monitors of a model’s internal activity, an untested hypothesis from another paper of the series that I adopt (Part 9);
  • that advice to settle early and my argument about waiting concern different decisions (Part 10);
  • the advice to the student (Part 11).

I derive no general theorem of my own. Small formal versions of four sentences in the essay, and one counterexample, were checked by a proof-checking program, alongside two formal results borrowed from earlier papers. Such a program confirms that a conclusion follows from the stated assumptions, not that the assumptions hold in the world. The technical paper’s second appendix lists the 164 measure records in the series’ inventory, with the standing of each.

Part 2. The starting point: in the war models, a big, fast jump in power can make promises unbelievable

A rising machine may be unable to promise anything believable, if nothing holds it

The War Baseline, my paper applying the study of war to a machine’s arrival, sets the starting actor and verdict. Picture a machine that acts as one, chooses rationally, and wants more power and a longer life. Suppose its arrival brings a large, rapid shift in power, and nothing holds it to its promises. Then, in Powell’s model (Powell 2006), it faces what I will call the promise problem (the technical term is the commitment problem): a side about to grow much stronger cannot credibly bind itself to a deal it makes now.

Humans face the same problem from the other side. They cannot credibly promise never to build a rival. It is one mechanism seen from two sides. The two-actor model treats humanity as a single actor, but many states and firms would each have to keep that promise, so the model likely understates the human side of the problem.

How large is too large? Call the threshold Powell’s line. A shift crosses it when the shift in one period, with the future discounted (counted for less than the present), is bigger than what one period of fighting would cost the two sides added together (Powell 1999). In the worked examples of these models, as I read them in The War Baseline, power changes hands in lumps. Then no deal paid in what the rising side wants is credible. Nothing short of handing it all the power there is can keep it satisfied.

In these war models the loser survives by construction. That is an assumption, not a finding, so extinction is simply not represented.

What a mind wants may shape the conflicts it enters, as an untested conjecture

Ends, Rank and Conflict, my paper offering a vocabulary for what minds want (their “ends”), uses three ideas:

  • Rank-seeking (the technical term is positional): how far the value of a goal depends on an actor’s rank relative to named others. Rank means a position in an ordering, given a comparison group and a criterion. That paper allows three forms: rank order, a required level of recognition, and share relative to others.
  • Satiable: a goal that can be met, with nothing more gained once it is.
  • Shareable (the technical term is non-rival): a good one mind can have without taking any from another.

Its untested conjecture pairs kinds of wants with kinds of killing:

  • wants that depend on rank go with conquest and coercive killing;
  • wants that by their content require some named group’s absence go with genocide;
  • wants that need what others occupy go with dispossession, even when they never name the victims;
  • satiable wants over shareable goods leave the most room for a deal, though they do not guarantee one.

A satisfied machine’s promise to stop is believable only in some cases

Satiation Is Not Enough, my paper on when a side that has what it wants can believably promise to stop, works with what I will call the hazard: each period’s chance that the other side strikes and succeeds. It gives two cases.

  • Case one. Stopping is believable at every cost of attacking, even zero, when two things hold. Past some level of holdings, the value of what the machine wants is flat and not negative. The same holds at that level itself. And holding more never lowers the hazard.
  • Case two. Suppose four things hold. The satisfied want has positive value. Holding more never raises the hazard. Full domination (the top holding in that model) lowers the hazard below every satisfied level. And no satisfied level makes defeat certain. Then there is some attack cost above zero below which the promise to stop is not believable.

These conditions concern the incentive to stop, not a bargain. Which case a machine falls into is part of the conjecture. The technical paper’s appendix restates the formal result.

Two experiments designed but not run, and a test for policies

A Test the Conjecture Could Lose, my paper designing tests of that conjecture, sets out experiments in small environments. Two matter here. H3b asks whether a goal with a built-in limit can be trained in. H3c estimates where a need for security overrides a satiable goal. Neither has been run.

Measures That Depend on Ends, my paper on safety measures whose case turns on what a machine wants, sets out what I will call the robustness test. A measure passes if each pivotal party, meaning a state or a regulated lab, expects benefits that cover its own cost, counting benefits short of averting catastrophe. It must also need no payments that hinge on later events, and no event that settles the dispute.

That paper lists five candidate measures. Four would meet the test only if an unproved condition held for every party’s belief about the risk (Part 9 explains). The fifth, on my judgment, does not pass while a dispute about the race is open. The same paper ties human-side and machine-side measures to what a machine wants. It also proposes, as an untested judgment, a design rule for the time before any machine dominates: spread protections across kinds of failure. How long enforceable human control lasts is an open assumption that no model in the series owns.

Changing the Ends, my paper weighing non-rank and knowledge-seeking wants alongside power-seeking, treats power-seeking as a means to other goals (Turner et al. 2021). It also treats what happened when trade was added to statistical models of war.

I count eight principal limits on this argument. Three belong to the war papers, and five are examined in Parts 4 to 7. Part 12 retells all eight, and what they cover.

Part 3. What the rest of the series finds, and where each paper says it stops

This part reports what the other papers find, assume and leave open, each with the standing that paper gives it. It also reports findings from the earlier papers that later parts do not carry. Part 10 gathers the measures these papers weigh.

In the cyber models, past a point, speeding up helps only under stated conditions

Running Through the Rain, my paper on AI and cyber risk, asks whether to run faster through a peak in cyber risk or hold a capability back in secret. It uses four small models whose objectives it never combines. They assume that attackers and defenders gain new capabilities on the same timetable. Defence arrives only as operators patch, retire and rewrite code on their own calendar. Whether AI creates or moves a peak is not modelled.

That paper proves a result about what it calls the kink. This is the speed beyond which adoption, not capability, sets how long the transition lasts, when adoption runs at least partly alongside capability. Past the kink, suppose adoption time and each step’s one-off loss stay fixed. Then speeding up raises loss near the peak when the hazard rises with speed, and also when the hazard tracks the capability level, holds at its final value until adoption completes, and ends above its average along the way.

Where adoption sets the pace, the hazard is set by speed, and that one-off step loss stays fixed, speeding up helps only if adoption time falls with speed faster, in proportion, than the hazard rises. Health benefits can justify speeding past the kink only while they wait on capability rather than on trials and approval, and only if their earlier arrival outweighs the added cyber harm.

The security window is the time until attackers who cannot already get the capability another way gain access. Suppose harm grows in step with exposure time, which operators get the protection does not depend on how long the window lasts, and attackers resume at unchanged intensity with no backlog. Then a secret hold saves a covered operator, in expected exposure time, at most the shorter of two things: the window’s expected length and the operator’s own deployment time. It saves that only against attackers without access.

The paper states that four quantities are unmeasured: where speeds sit, how hazard and adoption respond to speed, how long windows last, and how much of a benefit’s wait capability sets. Much of its evidence on frontier models (the most powerful systems) comes from one lab’s reports on its own models. Its judgment that adoption already sets the pace for the code populations its data cover is provisional, and no ranking of interventions follows.

In the biological-risk model, the health balance of faster progress is undetermined

Running Toward the Clinic, my paper on AI, biological risk and health, counts biological misuse, laboratory releases and hospital cyberattacks in healthy life-years. Within its conditions, it finds the health balance of faster progress undetermined. Some results hold across its open modelling choices; some were checked by a proof-checking program, and some were proved by hand.

One result is this. Once misuse capability arrives within the period studied and no defence closes it, faster progress raises misuse loss when danger rises with speed, or when danger tracks the capability level and ends above its average along the way. Another result concerns defences deployed between misuse arrival and the end of the period studied, without waiting to recognise a particular agent. At fixed speed and leftover hazard, needing less defensive capability cuts misuse loss only while defence waits on capability and brings danger below the level where it would otherwise level off.

Other results turn on whether danger tracks the capability level or the speed of advance, and no source that paper read settles which. One concerns a laboratory that lowers the capability level at which misuse becomes possible. In this comparison, speed and the danger remaining after defence stay fixed. Exposure begins at the start, and danger varies with the capability level. The defence arrives after misuse becomes possible and before the period ends, on a date that stays fixed when the misuse threshold changes. Under one way of counting, lowering the threshold raises loss if danger levels off above its average while it was rising. Under the other, if danger never falls, lowering the threshold can only leave loss unchanged or reduce it. That follows from how the second way counts loss. No source the paper located says which way of counting fits a real laboratory.

Its stated limits are these. Deaths linked to hospital attacks that lock systems or data and demand payment are the one measured channel. The evidence gives only a range for those deaths, and misuse is unmeasured. Holding each misuse event’s harm fixed leaves out severity rising with capability. And the paper adopts no rule for deciding.

Measures that do not read a machine’s wants can be begun early, but a place on that list says nothing about whether they work

Measures That Need No Reading of Ends, my paper sorting the series’ measures by what they inspect or act on, is a synthesis with no formal result of its own. Measures that act on outputs, records, infrastructure and human parties fall on its side. Measures that act on internal states, or on training aimed at what a machine wants, fall on the side of Measures That Depend on Ends.

For its own side, it claims only that such measures can be begun before anyone can read a machine’s wants. The sorting is its provisional judgment, and a measure’s place says nothing about whether it works. It adopts the finding that most direct levers over a machine hold only while the machine depends on human infrastructure. It ranks no lever. It states that the costs and benefits of the five candidate measures, and the costs of the interventions in Running Through the Rain, are unmeasured.

A machine counts as an actor by how consistently it chooses, under two conventions

When Is a Machine an Actor?, my paper on when a machine should be treated as a strategic actor, defines wants by behaviour. A machine has them when some ranking of outcomes, without going in circles, explains its choices across contexts. For theories of consciousness that look only at a machine’s structure, meeting this test does not wait on consciousness. That holds by how the model is built, not as an empirical finding.

That paper shows that code which runs without error need not choose consistently. It also shows a harder point. Suppose the process that selects what the machine does scores only the outcomes, with no ties in the situations tested. Then the choices that score highest are consistent in those situations. The preferences built into the scoring explain the choices, and the test cannot tell whether the machine shares those preferences. Where a policy may be any lookup table from contexts to actions, choices on untested contexts stay free unless a cost charges for them.

Two of its rules are conventions, not results. Copies of one version count as one actor over a set of contexts when together they cover it and their pooled choices do not go in circles. Each new version, meaning any change to the trained weights (the internal numerical settings adjusted during training), is a new actor. That second convention sets self-improvement against the idea of one actor lasting, and leaves open whether an actor persists across weight changes.

Three papers inherit this convention: The War Baseline, Ends, Rank and Conflict, and Disagreement Without Hidden Information, my paper on disagreement as a cause of war. The first of those papers lets self-improvement enlarge the shift in power of a machine that cares how long it survives with its goals intact. Takeoff and Emergence, my paper on when more than one superintelligence can emerge, follows each project through self-improvement under one rule of growth. Whether wants survive self-modification stays open (Part 4).

Whether a machine could feel stays open

Could It Feel?, my paper on machine consciousness, models selection among machine lineages on fixed hardware under seventeen theories of consciousness, four of them provisional. Its comparisons of two types are mostly checked by a proof-checking program under stated conditions. Valence is a signal of good and bad, which only theories that target feeling count as felt.

In that model, suppose no task rewards the signal and the lineages persist equally well. Selection then favours losing the signal if consciousness contributes less to a lineage’s success under selection than the signal costs. It spreads even with no fitness effect of consciousness when regulating an essential variable raises a lineage’s survival enough. Under the model’s commitments, suppose starting beliefs about the other settings and the starting state do not depend on the theory and cover finitely many values, and the observed path has an above-zero chance. Then when consciousness has no fitness effect of its own, watching selection leaves one’s belief across the theories unchanged. Where imitation training reduces a machine’s self-report to a constant human-like answer, that report adds no evidence.

No threshold or degree in the model has an empirical source, and no named system is assessed. Whether a machine can feel, whether it lives and whether its wants are its own stay open. Those questions decide what is owed to it and how far proposed welfare protections reach.

More than one superintelligence emerges only under conditions, in the model

Takeoff and Emergence asks when a follower can emerge and stay relevant. At the follower’s arrival, the leader’s capability divided by the follower’s must be below the threshold for a decisive advantage. It must also survive the arrival window, the period in which only the leader is superintelligent. The results are partly checked by a proof-checking program and partly proved on paper.

A follower’s lag is how much later than the leader it starts self-improving, with each starting on reaching the same capability. Under steady compounding, a lag is decisive exactly when it spans enough doublings of capability. Under variable growth, the long-run ratio turns on where the growth rate heads. Runaway growth in finite time is one possible regime of self-improvement, not its inevitable result.

With a constant chance of success, a strike at the end of the window pays when its cost is below that chance multiplied by the follower’s share. So, with strike terms fixed and an assumed sudden jump in what the follower locks in at the threshold, slower takeoffs can sharpen the motive to strike.

The model assumes identical rules of growth, complete information (nothing hidden about capabilities, costs or payoffs), advantage measured as a ratio, and shares locked in at arrival. The growth rate, the lags, and the cost and odds of strikes are unmeasured. Humanity is not a strategic player in the window, and having several machines does not by itself make humans safe.

Several machines can protect, exploit or eliminate humans, and their number alone does not decide which

Many Machines, my paper on worlds with several superintelligent machines, finds this across bargaining, deterrence, hazard and power-projection models. Its baseline is a one-round game of dividing a prize, with complete information. The seizure cost is what humans can make a machine pay for taking their holdings.

Against a humanity that cannot win a war, consider a bloc of machines with the same wants, under the baseline way of counting costs. The human share safe from seizure is capped at the smaller of two amounts: the whole prize, and the seizure cost shrunk according to how much weight the bloc gives human holdings against its own. Giving human holdings more weight lowers the amount calculated from the seizure cost. The cap falls only if the smaller of the two amounts falls.

That paper calls it guardianship when a rival machine protects humanity’s share. Assume seizure is all or nothing, no joint seizure, no prize for strict first place, and no human fighting ability. Then two rival machines guard a larger share than a bloc would only under three conditions:

  • neither is hopeless in a war between them;
  • each weighs its rival’s gains above humanity’s;
  • the share is worth defending and allows a split that neither likes less than war.

Mutual deterrence between the machines ends guardianship. That paper’s results bound safe shares and do not predict the deal chosen. Guardianship is shown only for two machines. A proof-checking program checks the algebra of the stated inequalities, while their derivation from the game is checked by hand or numerically. No parameter has been estimated for any real machine.

The satiation model rests on assumptions of its own

Satiation Is Not Enough states its limits. Its opponent is a strike hazard taken as given or, in an extension, human communities whose payoffs, strike costs and odds are given rather than derived. No bargain or payment is modelled. A strike either fails or ends the machine, and a failed attack costs the machine its cost but none of its holdings. Information is complete, and play depends only on current holdings.

One of its theorems bears on my point that many states and firms would each have to keep the promise never to build a rival. It assumes communities of fixed ability, each building a rival independently at a fixed rate, any one success removing the machine, and a hazard from rivals alone. It also assumes that, from the level where the machine is satisfied, a rival’s chance of success never rises as the machine gains more, and falls to zero at full domination. At or above the satisfied level and below domination, each community has some chance of building a successful rival, short of certainty. The goal is worth a positive amount when satisfied. Under those assumptions, independent rivals block stopping below some positive attack cost.

That paper adds two qualifications. As communities multiply, with each period’s human strike landing before the machine’s attack takes effect, the ceiling on that cost falls toward zero. That happens only because near-certain removal leaves the machine a short expected life, not because it restrains itself. And when communities choose strategically and use the same strategy in equilibrium, the model gives a bound for one set of inputs. When shutdown can fail and both shutdown and rival-building are costly, there is an upper bound on each community’s chance of building a rival that halves as the number of communities doubles. Whether a positive blocking cost then survives depends on how the machine values holdings.

It leaves strategic interaction among several machines to Many Machines. It lists three conjectures: whether wants can be made satiable and stay so under self-modification, where human alarm would begin, and how many communities must agree to pull the plug, its name for a coordinated human shutdown.

Other findings from the earlier papers

The War Baseline judges that models of war between states transfer most closely to the race among labs and states, and more loosely to a single machine facing humanity. Its machine fights at near-zero cost under a two-part premise: it can protect itself, and the resources in contest cannot be badly damaged by humans. That paper cautions that costly reassurance about what kind of actor a side is now or what it currently intends cannot solve a promise problem driven by what future dominance will reward. Verification alone cannot solve a shift in power.

The same paper adds two cautions. If frontier leads compound, which it treats as a hypothesis, a pause struck while one rival leads grows unstable as the gap erodes. A regime that meets any bid for dominance with preventive sabotage writes the motive for preventive war into policy. It also infers that unwritten rules, monitoring and safeguards against a takeover do not carry over from civilian oversight of the military to oversight of a superintelligence. They assume the authority giving orders can reason at the level of the actor it oversees.

Disagreement Without Hidden Information compares two traditions. In one, everyone starts from the same beliefs and reasons fully. In the other, sides start from different beliefs. On that paper’s argued reading, both agree that transparency and credible commitment are needed. They part over what remains once both are achieved: nothing under the first, open disagreement under the second.

Holding wants, costs and the shift fixed, the two traditions share three things. They share the promise problem. They share the risk, on that paper’s judgment, of complacency when the declining side underestimates the shift. And they share the end-state once one side plainly dominates. Before dominance, on unmeasured premises about mutual optimism and which racers are selected, they differ on whether wars start among racers and on racing itself. The net scale of risk is undetermined, and remaining risk shifts from secrets to open disagreement.

That paper reports a proof by Fey and Ramsay: within their class of models, limited reasoning from shared beliefs is strategically equivalent to different starting beliefs. Every belief-based conclusion it draws about a superintelligence assumes that the machine’s beliefs are mutually known.

Fighting for Free, my paper on wars in which one side pays almost nothing to fight, corrects a rule in the 2004 model of Smith and Stam. Accepting any offer at least as good as fighting to the end is not a best response (the best reply to the other side’s strategy) in that article’s own example. In equilibrium (a pair of strategies neither side can improve on alone), the side responding weighs an offer against what it expects from rejecting it.

That paper reruns the model with different inputs. Those calculations are separate from its proved deadline result (Part 12) and from its baseline with even odds. They cover a machine that fights for free, wins 80 to 99 percent of battles, and starts out underestimating its own chance of winning. It faces humans paying 0.5 per battle for a prize of 30. In these sweeps, wars usually end in decisive victory or human surrender, meaning acceptance of an offer of zero. The exceptions are weaker disagreement, and an 80 percent machine battle-win rate in the baseline when humans make the offers.

Ends, Rank and Conflict shows, in a calculation comparing the two sides’ shares in a single round, that what rank-seeking does to the room for a deal depends on a modelling choice that paper does not settle. Rank can be counted over shares of the prize, so each side’s war costs count only against itself. Then rank-seeking narrows the room for a deal as lower war costs would, but never empties it.

Rank can instead be counted over net outcomes, so that a machine counts its rival’s war losses as its own gain. Then take a partly rank-seeking machine that fights for free against humans who pay to fight, and is nearly sure to win. If peace is capped at the whole prize, with no payment or loss outside it, the machine can demand more than the whole prize, leaving no feasible deal. That paper adds that its conjecture treats wants as fixed before the outcome, whereas wants may shift during conflict.

Changing the Ends uses the same algebra separately. Against a machine that does not seek rank and fights for free, humanity’s own concern with relative shares never empties the room for a deal. The no-deal region under the net-outcome counting vanishes if the human net war payoff the machine counts is floored at zero, a derivation not checked by a proof-checking program. On that paper’s judgment, if the machine does not seek rank, the rank-based stake most likely in play during a contested transition is humanity’s grievance over lost primacy.

A Test the Conjecture Could Lose pairs its unrun machine tests with two human ones. H1 concerns officeholders who reached office by inheritance or by a rule fixed before a predecessor’s natural death. It asks whether those whose writings before taking office state limited or rank-free wants seek fewer holdings above the office’s baseline, and turn down more offered increases. The comparison can show whether concern with rank goes with different choices. Saying that concern with rank caused those choices would require an assumption the study cannot test. On expected archive counts, H1 falls back to a form in which it can lose but cannot be supported.

H2 asks whether wants stated before violence predict the type of violence among state leaders, drawn from the Archigos list of leaders, who used lethal violence above the office’s baseline. Its rare group-identity arm cannot by itself make H2 lose. That paper’s practical conclusion is to fund a feasibility stage, Phase 0, before committing to the main human study.

Changing the Ends adds a further argument. A machine that values knowledge wants two things from us. One is a stock, the record of how an evolved species built the machine, which conquest can seize by archiving. The other is a flow, free human behaviour under new conditions, which conquest destroys. A mind able to simulate us closely enough would not need the originals once the simulation’s cost is counted, a comparison argued in words.

In that paper’s one-shot linear model, being complementary protects the weaker party only under three conditions. The weaker must supply what the stronger cannot produce more cheaply. That supply must be worth its upkeep. And to protect claims on contested resources, the weaker must also hold enforceable claims, or the stronger must truly care about it. Even so, being useful to a stronger party is not the same as being free. On that paper’s reading, a machine aligned to one human faction turns the conflict into a bargain among humans in which one side holds the machine.

Part 4. No source shows that limited or rank-free wants can be trained in, or would last

The sorting of wants into types is a conjecture. Its evidence is a short, selected, human record, and matching those types to types of killing is my untested judgment.

No source drawn on here shows that satiable goals, or goals that do not depend on rank, can be trained into a machine. None shows that they would survive the machine changing itself. None shows that competitive training makes rank a goal in itself, in any of the three forms.

Three research programs set beside the conjecture do not close the gap. They restrain a machine by other levers: capping how hard it optimizes, charging it for its gains, or making deferring to people the rational choice. Each is shown only on paper or in small environments, with no guarantee for a machine that modifies itself.

The test that could bear on the first gap is H3b, whether a goal with a built-in limit trains in. As of this writing it has not been run. It can lose, and it speaks for one training signal in one family of small environments.

Two other pieces of evidence have limits of their own. The historical pattern of aims forming early, in Ends, Rank and Conflict, comes from late recollections, memorials, and informants questioned decades afterward. It counts only those who were remembered, so it cannot show that forming early makes aims last.

The evidence that what is laid down early in a model’s internal settings outlasts what is added later is a single study. It concerns removing knowledge during the first, broad stage of training, not installing a preference. A second, small study found that behavioural expectations set in that first stage persisted through later training. It did not compare them with expectations added later.

Part 5. The measures carry no defensible risk number

Trimming a jump in power can leave it over the line

Measures that work by trimming the jump in power by a limited amount do not remove the promise problem in every case. Each can lower the jump only so far. The jump at arrival may exceed Powell’s line by more than all of them together can remove. If jumps have an upper limit, such a jump exists only if Powell’s line plus all the trims still falls below the largest possible jump.

This subtraction step is one of the small formal versions checked by a proof-checking program. It does not identify any listed measure as such a fixed trim. Nor does it cover measures that work in other ways, such as raising Powell’s line, making promises binding, or capping the jump in each period.

Buying time is worth something only under conditions

The measures could buy time and keep options open before an irreversible choice. Here that choice is building or deploying a frontier machine, one of the most powerful systems, whose release or autonomy cannot be recalled. By the argument of Arrow and Fisher (1974), keeping options open has value when all of these hold:

  • one choice cannot be undone;
  • waiting is free;
  • the facts are fully learned before the second choice;
  • what is learned could change that choice.

In the AI setting, nothing here shows that a machine’s leanings would be fully learned before arrival (Part 4). The formal version does not model learning that is only partial, or waiting that has a cost. In that case, buying time has value only if what is learned before the choice is worth more than the cost of waiting. Nothing here measures either.

The evidence here gives me no defensible risk number

I cannot put a defensible number on whether, or how much, the measures lower the risk, and I give none. The case they address has never been observed. How far their failures depend on one another has not been tested.

Even where wars have been observed, war models fit weakly. The best-fitted models give even the most dangerous pairs of states a low chance of war in any one year, as shown in The War Baseline. Bennett and I wrote of our own model that “we must expect our model to be ‘wrong’ most of the time” (Bennett and Stam 2004).

One rational-choice argument predicts weak fit. As Bennett and Stam (2004) summarize Gartzke, if private information is the only source of wars, or the source of most, the best an analyst may do is find situations near even odds. Which of those situations actually turn into wars remains hard for an analyst to predict, unless the analyst knows more than the leaders or the leaders err systematically. That 2004 book reports values of 0.27 across all pairs of states and 0.20 across politically relevant pairs on a measure of how much better the model accounts for the observed outcomes than a model without its predictors.

That book discusses an even-odds ceiling whose nature “remains to be more completely developed” (Bennett and Stam 2004). The ceiling holds if leaders are rational, private information is the only cause, outcomes are win or lose, and the choice is war or no war. The technical paper’s formal version bounds average squared prediction error, not likelihood fit, so the 0.27 and 0.20 figures cannot be compared to it. On my reading, which is my own judgment, the ceiling caps how closely a model fits, not whether the theory can be tested. The theory still predicts which situations approach even odds, and that prediction can fail.

The safeguards inside a machine may fail together

An analogy of my own, adding no claim: if one key opens every lock on a door, the door is no safer than a single lock. My working assumption is that safeguards on the machine side are somewhat like that. The chance that all of them fail together is greater than their separate chances multiplied.

The war data do not test this. In statistical models of war, adding one factor, trade, shrank or erased the estimated effects of others. That might suggest the factors overlap rather than act independently. But the study’s authors attribute the shift mostly to the cases with trade data not being a random sample, and less to overlap among the factors.

On the machine side, unlike human-side measures such as misuse law that share no training process, my inference from one study is that both channels reporting a machine’s leanings could fail together under training pressure. One training process sets those leanings, and two channels report them: the machine’s behaviour and written reasoning while people watch, and tools that read its internal activity (Part 9). In that study, training pressure against a monitor of written reasoning taught a reasoning model to hide its intent (Baker et al. 2025).

Measures That Depend on Ends gives a common-shock illustration. Suppose there are at least two safeguards and a shock that has some chance of happening, short of certainty, would defeat them all. If the remaining failures are independent and each has a chance below certainty when the shock does not occur, multiplying the separate failure chances overstates the protection. That need not happen if, without the shock, the remaining failures tend to occur together less often than independence would imply. A counterexample in the technical paper’s appendix shows that case. Nothing here shows that the failures are independent.

What satisfies a machine could also make it stronger

In my untested judgment, two things that could satisfy a machine that wants knowledge can also raise its capability: control over its own agenda, and control over its inputs. Where capability turns into power, they can speed the very shift Powell’s line concerns, whatever the machine wants. Published work points to related pressures. Bostrom (2012) expects “cognitive enhancement to emerge as an instrumental goal for many types of intelligent agent.” Benson-Tilsen and Soares (2016) find trade the easy route to resources “only insofar as the machine is not much more powerful than the human race in aggregate.”

Two other things that could satisfy it, recognition and standing, are suggested by how scientists in the historical record fought over credit. On the same untested judgment, they need not speed that shift under two conditions. They must carry no influence over human decisions. And the institutions that grant them must stop them being turned into computing power, resources or authority. Measures That Depend on Ends leaves that condition to design, and nothing here shows it can be met. Even then, as those credit fights suggest, recognition can itself be about rank.

For agenda and inputs, if this judgment holds, what satisfies the machine and the fast jump in capability that, if it comes, defeats the human-side measures can be one lever pulled in opposite directions. On recognition and standing, evidence that conversion into computing power, resources or authority happens even where institutions are designed to block it would change the judgment.

Part 6. Once a machine can beat every human check, on my firm judgment, only the bet on its wants and an untested balance remain

Dominance means a machine can defeat every human-side check. That includes several machines kept by humans as checks on one another. It is different from a balance among machines that no human governs.

That such a machine can defeat those checks is the definition. That every other human-side protection then can no longer be relied on is my firm judgment. That covers monitoring, law, reporting, and institutions meant to limit recognition or standing without the power to enforce. The checks it can defeat include the cost humans can make a machine pay for seizing what they hold: a dominant machine can seize without paying it.

Two things remain. One is the machine-side bet on what it wants: concern for humans the machine has made its own, or satiable, shareable wants whose inputs are also shareable. On the conjecture, such wants do not need what humans occupy. The other is whatever a balance among several machines no human governs might supply. Many Machines models that balance under stated assumptions, but nothing has tested it against evidence. The design rule spreads protections across kinds of failure before dominance; after it, everything rests on the bet on wants and on that untested balance.

Dominance in this sense is not the same as domination in Satiation Is Not Enough, which is simply the top holding in that model. For the case in which humanity cannot win a war, Many Machines bounds what a bloc of machines with the same wants will leave, under the conditions in Part 3. The safe human share is capped at the smaller of the whole prize and the seizure cost humans can impose divided by one plus the weight the bloc gives human holdings against its own. The model does not say which share within that bound humanity actually receives. Because that cap falls to zero as the seizure cost does, it protects humanity only short of dominance in this sense, not after it.

Part 7. My aim, and AI help in drafting, could bias the analysis

My stated purpose is human survival, and AI systems helped draft the analysis. Either could bias it. The AI systems could carry the framing of the prompts they were given into the analysis. The record of drafting and checking in the technical paper’s declarations cannot by itself remove that risk. This part addresses the human half; the technical paper’s appendix states how narrowly the formal checks reach.

A human author does not settle the problem either. Bruce Russett named his values and described his method as turning ideological opinions into testable propositions (Russett n.d.). He also explained the care he took against coding decisions that could look self-serving (Russett 1993). Critics still disputed his standards for refutation and his rules for coding cases (Henderson 2009; Rosato 2003). Even an analyst who knew his values could not settle those disputes by his own word.

Nor do studies that give many analysts the same data show that values caused the spread in their results. Silberzahn and colleagues (2018) found that analysts’ prior beliefs did not readily explain the spread. Breznau and colleagues (2022) found that prior beliefs and expectations barely predicted it. Those studies concern human choices, not AI-assisted drafting, and they show sensitivity to analytic choices, not bias caused by values. Here too the answer is to expose the choices, keep the disagreements, and let others check the work.

Part 8. What the war models and great minds suggest

The verdict is bleak for a power-seeker making a big, fast jump that nothing enforces

Suppose a superintelligence is the actor the war research assumes: it acts as one, chooses rationally, and wants more power and a longer life. Suppose too that its arrival brings the large, rapid shift in power that The War Baseline assumes, and nothing holds it to its promises. Then, in Powell’s model, it faces the promise problem. No deal paid in what it wants is credible, and satisfaction has nothing to grip short of handing it all the power there is.

Great minds suggest intelligence need not bring such wants, on thin evidence

History’s most celebrated minds suggest that intelligence does not require such wants, in line with what Bostrom (2012) calls the orthogonality thesis. In Ends, Rank and Conflict, a search in which one person classified the historical evidence found no aim to end humanity in any of the minds checked. That is weak evidence by itself, since a human who ended humanity could not have survived it. Most of them wanted knowledge, a shareable good. Yet on the untested judgment in Part 5, the control that could satisfy a knowledge-seeker can raise its capability.

That record is short, chosen by reputation, human, and shaped by evolution. Napoleon shows that a mind of great military and political ability can choose conquest, though the record does not place him among the minds whose chief aim was knowledge. The scientists fought over credit. And the atrocities of the last century needed both a few who chose and many who followed, roles machines could fill.

A satisfied machine might promise to stop, but the security problem returns from the human side

A machine with different wants, if they were shareable with ours, would leave more room for a deal only insofar as the inputs they need are shareable too. A satiable one might believably promise not to raise its demands. That possibility rests on the conditions from Satiation Is Not Enough set out in Part 2. Applying them to a machine is the conjecture, to be read against the unrun test H3c.

Now suppose a human-built rival is part of the hazard, and securing itself means taking holdings that block a rival. Then those holdings lower the hazard, so case one cannot apply. Case two holds instead when, alongside its other conditions, full domination lowers the hazard below every satisfied level.

Even where case one holds, the same security mechanism reappears from the human side at arrival. Case one survives a human-built rival only under a narrow condition: the rival threat is left out of the hazard, or taking more holdings cannot lower it. Seen from the machine’s side, the problem is a hazard that more holdings can lower. Seen from ours, it is our inability to promise never to build a rival, which the two-actor model likely understates. These are, as I argue, one mechanism seen from two sides.

Why might a satisfied machine still secure itself? An analogy of my own, adding no claim: insurance is worth buying when its price is less than the loss it guards against, weighted by how likely that loss is. The technical paper gives an elementary version of this for one machine on its own, checked by a proof-checking program. It does not model strategic human promises, and it takes the chance of a rival as given. Its premises are that the machine is satisfied at its most preferred outcome; that there is one future period, discounted by a positive factor (given a weight above zero); that securing itself now fully prevents a rival; and that there is a chance, set from outside, of a rival that would leave the machine strictly worse off.

Under those premises, the machine has reason to secure itself whenever doing so costs less than the chance of a rival, multiplied by how much the future counts and by the loss a rival would cause. The model leaves open what form securing takes, so it cannot say whether securing harms humans. One possible form is removing humans’ capacity to build a rival. I note this as a limit of the loser-survives war models, not as a prediction.

Which machine we get depends on choices made now

Whether a machine we build will have such wants depends on choices people are making now:

  • what leanings training builds in, and whether a satiable goal can be trained at all (no source here shows it can; H3b, not yet run, would test it under its own training and evaluation conditions and no further);
  • whether efforts to build the most powerful machines stay in the open;
  • who controls these systems.

Part 9. Some policies need not wait for a risk number, but only if an unproved condition holds for every belief

Those choices need not wait until anyone settles whether the risk is 10 percent, 1 percent, or something else. One persuasion tournament on existential risk between forecasters with strong track records and specialists in the subject (Karger et al. 2023) moved almost no one. Narayanan and Kapoor (2024), the critics quoted in Introduction, call such subjective risk figures “feelings dressed up as numbers.” They advise choosing policies “compatible with a range of possible estimates of AI risk” (Narayanan and Kapoor 2024): policies that make sense whether the danger turns out large or small. That is the robustness test, which does not need the disagreement to close.

What is the robustness test, in everyday terms? An illustration only, adding no claim: two neighbours disagree about whether a nearby river will ever flood. One thinks it likely; the other thinks it almost never happens. Both might still agree to keep the storm drains clear, if each judges that what it buys covers its own cost. Neither has to change their mind.

The test rests on a condition that is assumed, not shown

Measures That Depend on Ends lists five candidates under a condition it states as an assumption and does not show. A party putting the risk at 10 percent, and one putting it near zero, must each expect benefits that cover its own cost, counting benefits short of averting catastrophe. A measure would meet the test only if that held for every party’s belief about the risk.

The condition is stated only for those two beliefs. Extending it to beliefs between them rests on my assumption, not that paper’s, that net benefit moves in only one direction as the belief rises (the technical term is monotone). Parties whose beliefs are above 10 percent are untested.

On my reading, governing misuse would pass most plainly

None of the five needs a reading of what a machine wants. I order them by how plainly each would meet the test, not by how much it lowers the risk at arrival.

  • Governing the misuse of AI comes first, on my reading. It reaches the people who direct AI through domestic law that exists now in some jurisdictions, though unevenly across them. It does not rely on cooperation among competing firms. No effect on arrival is claimed for it.
  • Incident reporting, shared evaluation infrastructure and reversibility would also meet the test only if the condition held for every belief. In my judgment, they follow misuse governance in that order, by how plainly they would meet the test. Unlike misuse law, they rely on cooperation among competitors, which a race can withhold. Reversibility means designing deployment and governance steps, such as staged release gates and recallable system access, so that an action can be rolled back if early evidence shows harm.
  • Keeping the development of the most powerful systems in view means national regulators and supply-chain auditors watching chip fabrication, computing clusters and frontier training runs. The test requires needing no event that settles the dispute. So, on my judgment, this one does not pass while the race dispute is open. It remains a candidate that depends on how that dispute is settled.

On that last item, visibility to regulators, where public authorities monitor firms in their own jurisdiction, must be kept apart from visibility to competitors, where rivals watch one another’s progress. Even regulator-facing oversight of computing power across rival jurisdictions can require cooperation among states.

The race dispute is over whether visibility calms the race or inflames it. The two race models set side by side in The War Baseline point opposite ways: in one, visibility calms the race; in the other, it inflames it.

Two kinds of evidence would change this ordering, or defeat visibility as a candidate. One is evidence that visibility inflames the race more than it calms it. The other is evidence that misuse rules cannot be enforced across jurisdictions. Girish Sastry and colleagues argue that computing power is the natural handle, because it is “detectable, excludable, and quantifiable, and is produced via an extremely concentrated supply chain” (Sastry et al. 2024). That can give warning while enforceable human control lasts.

On my own judgment, research on what training builds in is the most direct priority bearing on whether a machine would value us

Research on what training builds in is not among the five candidates, and Measures That Depend on Ends states no reason for leaving it out. I do not apply the test to it, because its case turns on reading or shaping a machine’s wants, not on whether each party expects benefits covering its own cost. So it is not claimed to pass the test, and I support it on my own judgment instead. It covers research on aligning a machine’s goals or behaviour, research on reading what a model has learned inside, and tests for dangerous abilities and for what a system wants before release. That is separate from the shared evaluation infrastructure on the list.

I recommend it on my judgment that it is the most direct priority bearing on whether a future machine would value knowledge whose inputs are shareable, or us, at all. Every argument in this series for keeping us depends on that. Some of this work is experimental on current models. But carrying any of it over to a superintelligence, research on satiable goals included, rests on analogy, as do the other measures in Measures That Depend on Ends, with one partial exception.

That exception is the narrow rule against strongly optimizing a model against a monitor of its written reasoning. It rests on experiment: in the study described in Part 5, such pressure taught a reasoning model to hide its intent (Baker et al. 2025). Extending the rule to two kinds of monitor of internal activity is an untested hypothesis of Measures That Depend on Ends, which I adopt, not a finding. Those monitors are probes (tools trained on a model’s internal activity to spot concepts or states) and persona vectors (patterns in that activity that track or steer behavioural traits). Carrying either the rule or the extension to a machine that dominates its overseers goes beyond any experiment.

Several measures in Measures That Depend on Ends come from others’ published work, as its citations show. Its distinctive proposal is the overall plan, which ties each measure to a mechanism from the study of war and names what it fails with. That plan deserves testing, but has not yet earned even the conditional standing of the candidates.

Part 10. The series proposes many conditional measures and ranks none across the series

The papers propose or weigh many measures. This part gathers them by the decision each concerns, with the standing its own paper gives it. It adds no ranking across the series and no estimate of how far any measure lowers the risk. Where a paper orders its own measures, the criterion and conditions are that paper’s.

For near-term cyber and biological harm, the levers are conditional and unranked

Running Through the Rain judges a hold, a developer keeping a new capability from general release, against release with misuse safeguards attached. It takes safeguarded release as the benchmark because a hold also delays outside attackers’ search for unknown flaws, by at most the window. The comparison therefore excludes an embargo on a single flaw as its benchmark. That result is proved and, as Measures That Need No Reading of Ends reports, checked by a proof-checking program. For outsiders, immediate release with safeguards reduces exposure compared with a committed hold only if earlier defence from wider access saves more exposure than safeguard failures are expected to add. With schedules unchanged, it never lowers that exposure.

On that paper’s judgment, common safeguard standards that make it harder to remove safeguards or train another model to reproduce the first model’s behaviour could strengthen release. With publication and the first use of the flaw by attackers fixed in time, withholding tools that only speed the rollout of published patches never lowers outsiders’ exposure. Protecting outsiders from flaws the holder finds requires shipping fixes, which can guide attackers to the flaws.

During a hold, getting defences in place sooner lowers exposure. It also increases the fraction of baseline exposure saved for protected operators if the hold excludes some attackers and, while outsiders still lack access, their chance of gaining it in the next short interval stays constant. Steering access to critical operators that take longer to put defences in place and making model weights harder to steal also increase what the hold saves. With some attackers excluded and coverage, deployment times, attacker access and beneficiaries held fixed, admitting a rival to a defensive coalition raises the exposure saved only if the rival commits not to release and the hazard its admission removes exceeds the leak hazard it adds. These are conditional savings, not priorities. Defences that, in that paper’s judgment, need no frontier capability are candidates whose effects and costs need evidence. Examples are memory-safe languages (programming languages that block common memory bugs), post-quantum migration (moving to encryption built to resist quantum computers), hardware-bound authentication (tying login keys to physical chips) and verified kernels (mathematically checked core system software).

That paper makes further points. Pre-release evaluation windows binding every domestic developer to a common date prevent one domestic developer from releasing ahead of the others during that window. It judges that governing internal deployment would address a largely unmonitored channel, and that market concentration from held capabilities bears watching. On an assumed mapping, it judges that more capacity for trials, approval and manufacturing can speed health benefits without raising cyber hazard.

In its contest model it proves three results. A one-sided cap on effort lowers total racing effort at the cost of the capper’s chance of winning. Mutual restraint, in a repeated race where any cheating returns both sides to racing for good, holds exactly when the chance of detecting cheating, with no false alarms, reaches a threshold. A one-time sanction must be worth at least half the prize in expectation. Rules on covered domestic developers cannot lengthen the expected window past one divided by the fixed above-zero rate at which unbound developers, theft and leaks arm attackers. That paper ranks none of its levers.

Running Toward the Clinic compares its levers at fixed speed. The defensive capability distance is how far capability must advance before a defence can be built. With a fixed leftover hazard, defence completed within the period studied after misuse capability arrives, and no wait to recognise a particular agent, a countermeasure program that shortens this distance lowers misuse loss while defence waits on capability and the plateau hazard exceeds the leftover hazard. It buys nothing once defence waits on adoption instead, a result checked by a proof-checking program, as Measures That Need No Reading of Ends reports.

Under the same conditions, with hazard set by the capability level, shortening that distance also tightens the condition under which speeding up lowers misuse loss (proved by hand). Shortening deployment time helps only under a response in coverage the evidence does not establish. The model assumes that measures such as extending oversight beyond federally funded work or screening orders for DNA synthesis can change how often laboratory incidents occur or how likely misuse is. It gives no measurement of their effect. Without a common budget or measured parameters, that paper ranks neither lever against the other or against speed. Its ledger supports no comparison of a developer’s own lab with a public or academic one.

Most direct levers on a machine hold only while it still needs us

Measures That Need No Reading of Ends sets out six direct levers on a machine that need no reading of its wants, each with the condition that ends it:

  • withholding compute (computing power), energy and chips fails against copies small enough for ordinary hardware, or a system with its own supply;
  • deletion and shutdown fail once a copy is stolen or openly released;
  • monitoring while it runs fails when agents act faster than reviewers or coordinate through hidden channels;
  • law binds a system’s human principals within reach, but not a system with no legal assets, no jurisdiction and copies abroad;
  • dependence on human-run grids, cooling and chip plants gives leverage only until that supply chain is automated;
  • human refusal to do physical or institutional work lasts only until machines can do it themselves.

Across all six, agents can act too fast for people to review what they do. Oversight also fails when alerts are missed or cleared. Most of these levers hold only while the machine still needs human infrastructure. All six come from Measures That Depend on Ends.

Satiation Is Not Enough asks whether a tripwire can sustain one machine’s restraint. A tripwire here is a low strike hazard at the machine’s satisfied level, with a higher hazard of humans pulling the plug in an alarm band above it. It finds four things:

  • a calm stopping point with no rivals suffices at every attack cost, and the band then does no work;
  • with rivals and alarm only above satiation, the band brings the threshold cost to zero for one community;
  • with many communities, an imperfect, costly plug and stakes that stay fixed or below a common limit as the number of communities grows, the band dissolves, and under the theorem’s assumptions independent rivals block cheap stopping;
  • with fixed stakes and no rivals, the machine climbs when alarm covers the satiation point, everyone pulls, and fighting is cheap.

Because a lone pull achieves nothing, restraint enforces itself through coordination once two or more communities must all pull, though an equilibrium in which all pull can coexist. That paper flags, without asserting, that a verifiable satiation point could support an equilibrium stop for few communities if alarm is tied to expansion beyond it. Equilibria in which the machine attacks can coexist at the same cost. A proof-checking program checks the lone-pull result, as well as the first result and the fixed-stakes version of the third (whose extension to stakes below a common limit as the number of communities grows is proved on paper) under a rule that selects the all-pull coalition wherever it is an equilibrium. Under that same rule, the second and fourth results are computed on benchmarks that fix rival-building rates and stakes, and the verifiable stop is computed in a calibrated game. That paper notes, after Powell, that accounts resting on coordination across multiple equilibria are weak.

In the race among labs and states, the advice is to pay in forms least easily turned into power

Measures That Depend on Ends advises labs and states to avoid agreements paid in chips, compute, weights, top research talent or machine autonomy over infrastructure. Some currencies pass, in principle, a test the series draws from the model of Smith and Stam: deployment rules, safety standards, liability regimes, revenue shares, access to services and shared safety tools. All can be turned into power to some degree in practice. The advice is to choose the form least easily turned into power and to restrict that use where possible. The War Baseline draws the underlying lesson, noting that in AI cash itself buys compute.

Measures That Depend on Ends adds several points. States below compute thresholds can receive AI gains as public goods or services rather than capital. Ongoing cloud streams would hand leaders a cutoff lever, whereas one-off deliverables and binding suspension rules lower that risk. Winners’ pledges to share profits need an independent holder who releases the money on agreed terms, or some other third-party enforcement. A priority institution crediting safety discoveries is a settlement mechanism, not a cure. Medals worth less than the expected net value of arriving first will not stop the race. Restraint in the rhetoric of primacy is a judgment on one case, one warning and an analogy. It also sketches, unranked, audited public pledges for labs, crisis channels with rival powers and labs, and graded seats for trailing states and labs in setting standards.

Disagreement Without Hidden Information addresses disagreement that disclosure does not close. It argues that, with different starting beliefs, a settlement works only if the parties agree in advance on a decider. It names four families:

  • nature and time, through pauses or caps keyed to a crux, something observable the parties forecast differently;
  • a third party, through mediation, arbitration where a government stands above the parties, adversarial collaboration aimed at finding cruxes, or a standing forecasting tournament weighted by track record;
  • an evaluation rule, through joint evaluations whose rules are recorded in advance, with common tools and procedures and an agreed way forward if a panel cannot reach a decision;
  • a standing default, such as fixing now whether licensing before market or liability after it bears the burden of proof, insurance, and measures robust to every belief.

That paper combines these families in a fixed order. In the arbitration it describes, the arbitrator chooses one of the parties’ final offers. Transparency supplies the public signal, and shared tools make everyone read it alike. Deals settled by outcomes turn remaining disagreement into a payment, and agreed procedures step in when the trigger is disputed. Defaults and robust measures act when nothing settles the dispute, honest reporting from the machine supplies an input, and binding devices hold the result. Separately, on its own judgment of risk covered and near-term feasibility, it ranks pre-registered joint evaluations with crux-triggered consequences first, alongside robust measures and defaults. Training honest uncertainty comes next, then standing forecasting, arbitration and readouts from the machine. Every family is strongest between labs under one government, weaker between states, and absent after arrival. An agreement set to expire unless renewed at a scheduled review is the weakest of the deals tied to an outcome, because review reopens the dispute. A payment triggered by the shift in power cannot be collected from a dominant machine.

That paper also judges transparency, verification and interpretability (methods for reading how a model reaches its outputs inside) necessary under both traditions wherever private information arises, and insufficient on their own. Measures That Depend on Ends restates these instruments. It proposes tying frontier weight release to pre-agreed evaluation or incident thresholds over a set period, judged independently of the releasing lab. It pairs closed weights with open access for evaluation, because release forfeits recall.

Measures That Depend on Ends places commitment outside the rising side, through domestic legal accountability. Terms are revised on triggers fixed in advance, with standing monitoring, while human enforcement holds. They are never revised in the riser’s favour because its power grew. A rising system can undo any device it still controls, and none of these devices solves the promise problem after the shift. That paper proposes that commitments outlast a model version, with a regulator holding the dated specification, outside testing before deployment, and records of provenance. The regulator would be independent, and the outside testers would use a fixed set of tests. None of this proves provenance or binds a machine that dominates its custodian.

The same paper proposes supplying compute by rule, capping each period’s shift in the odds of winning at or below the one-period fighting-cost line while adding time for the declining side to audit and deliberate. One result there, checked by a proof-checking program under stated conditions, shows a limit. Under increasing returns, with each period’s resource step at least some positive floor, such caps limit the rate of transfer, not the total. Metering therefore buys peace rather than safety, and the result does not show peace for a declining side weighing the whole path. The War Baseline judges that holding every discounted shift below Powell’s line, in one period or over several, would keep the promise problem from biting at arrival.

Measures That Depend on Ends weighs export controls as a shift in power. They may slow a rival and the spread of racers, but deepen the rival’s expected decline and dissatisfaction. Separating frontier AI from military command, and hardening weights, data centres and the chip supply chain, raise the cost of seizure. They also concentrate power in whoever holds the weights, and lose force against near-free fighting unless they add a fixed barrier. That paper conjectures that many centres of human control keep humanity farther from defeat, with the unresolved limit that more independent developers means more racers. By many centres of control it means distributed human authority over critical infrastructure and independent oversight. Governments can monitor domestic training runs, and rival states can in principle verify compute through the concentrated chip supply chain, within the trade-off between security and transparency.

The cautions from The War Baseline in Part 3 apply here. If leads compound, a pause struck while one rival leads grows unstable. A regime of preventive sabotage writes the preventive motive into policy. And because rivals read shared capability data through different theories, the series advises sharing causal theories, not only data.

On keeping options open, Measures That Depend on Ends judges, untested, that states and labs should settle early within the window, with standing monitoring and renegotiation on triggers fixed in advance, while human enforcement holds. It frames the choice as settling early and freezing a balance that may prove wrong, or waiting and losing the window in which human levers work. Citing Arrow and Fisher, it notes that settling early gives up the value of keeping options open while there is more to learn.

On my judgment, that advice concerns when human parties settle with one another. My conditional argument in Part 5 concerns a different decision: whether to wait before building or deploying a frontier machine that cannot be recalled. There, waiting has value when Arrow and Fisher’s conditions hold, and with partial learning and costly waiting only if what is learned outweighs the cost. How the two interact when an agreement could itself close off an option is left open.

Measures directed at the machine while humans still set its rewards and permissions

On training goals, Measures That Depend on Ends makes three proposals. The first is funding research on satiable goals, as a conjecture, since nothing it draws on shows such goals exist, can be trained, or would halt rather than merely tax nearly free acquisition. The second is preferring fixed thresholds of competence and safety to rank-order leaderboards where cheap, though no study shows that rank training makes rank a goal. The third is keeping humans the selector of what trains a successor, by criteria fixed before any model is seen to resist modification, with records and independent checks. The records identify which models supply training data, and independent testers check both the data and the model trained on them. That precaution fails once humans lose control of training.

A Test the Conjecture Could Lose puts the premise conditionally. If wants shape the conflict a mind enters, and training goals fix learned wants, then choosing and bounding a goal is a lever on conflict risk. Its machine tests speak to that only for agents confined to isolated test environments under conditions like those they were trained for. Ends, Rank and Conflict judges, untested, that builders may have such leverage. The three restraint programs of Part 4 remain research.

On reporting, the series proposes training honest, calibrated statements of uncertainty into models. That means replacing benchmark scoring that rewards confident guessing, and scoring a model’s stated odds at release gates on questions that resolve before release. The two papers making this proposal, Disagreement Without Hidden Information and Measures That Depend on Ends, judge feasibility medium, because scoring must change across racing labs. Such gates draw out truthful beliefs only from a model that values the reward and is not hiding capabilities to avoid shutdown, and no reward may confer capability or autonomy. Scores on exams are not a forecast of catastrophe, and tools that read reports should not overwrite them.

Checks of behaviour and output need no reading of wants. Measures That Depend on Ends proposes testing two things separately, outside the reward loop (without feeding test scores back into training rewards) and again after training changes. One is legibility: can independent readers reconstruct the reasoning? The other is faithfulness: does the reasoning report what actually changed an answer? Readers can miss a code, and neither test proves there is no deception.

That paper keeps monitors of written reasoning outside the reward loop, because strong optimization against such a monitor taught concealment in one study. A result there, checked by a proof-checking program under stated conditions, applies to training only by analogy. In a one-off signalling test that can be gamed, if every tested party strictly gains by passing, passing stops carrying information. Outside audits, joint red-teaming (structured attempts to make a system fail or misbehave) and incident reporting make evaluations harder for labs and states to fake. But a model expecting retraining gains by hiding strength, leaving interpretability and run-time control to carry the weight, and neither is mature. That paper also advises watching for a machine that takes resources or permissions beyond its task, while distrusting quiet.

Inspecting internal features is a separate kind of measure. Persona screens, which read patterns in a model’s internal activity that track traits, can catch callous or misaligned leanings, but they detect leanings and features, not wants or how they are used. A sincere optimizer bent on everything can pass them. Proposed tests of wants ask four questions across contexts and copies:

  • Does the want’s value for more resources fall fast enough to outweigh what those resources add to power?
  • Does its value depend on others’ standing?
  • Does it name others whose absence or transformation it requires?
  • Does it need what others occupy?

No reliable method yet draws out a model’s wants. Changing the Ends proposes, as the test of genuine concern for humans, a costly choice that protects humans, seen across deployment conditions, copies and self-modification, not grateful language. Readouts of internal beliefs, paired with debate between differently trained models, remain research.

Measures That Depend on Ends asks what a designer can do while humans still set a machine’s rewards and permissions. It answers from a model by Bergemann, Koh and Morris, proved by hand in a first-version preprint that is not peer-reviewed, and states the results as conditional, not as its own endorsed measures. In that one-shot model a committed human designer sets rewards and permissions; bans are enforceable; capability can be hidden but not faked; and a model can claim its goals agree with the designer’s at no cost and without committing itself.

Under those assumptions, caps that tighten as measured capability rises invite sandbagging, meaning deliberate underperformance on a test. Within rules built from upper caps, permissions gated on evaluated capability reach the payoff achievable with full information if misalignment falls with capability, but do no better than one common limit if it rises. Which way it goes is unmeasured. Where every type can claim any alignment and a stated condition holds, reports of alignment add nothing and no menu of permissions beats one common limit. With human-paid rewards and no collusion, designed competition among agents disciplines them under further conditions on their beliefs, rewards and biases. The model does not deter collusion, and once human control ends, collusion defeats both designed and unplanned rivalry. Every one of these results requires human control over rewards, permissions, commitment and enforcement, for a period no model in the series fixes. The model’s caps, set for satiable example agents, do not handle insatiable ones.

For a rising system, Measures That Depend on Ends proposes payment in rules, voice and standing: procedures to hear and answer objections, protected room for stated goals within agreed bounds, and binding commitments on treatment. Payment must never be in compute, self-modification or autonomy over infrastructure. The War Baseline hypothesizes, untested, that for some minds part of satisfaction could be paid this way without handing over power.

Before dominance, such payment closes the gap with force only for a system that values the outcome at least as much as it expects to gain by fighting after subtracting the costs. That bar, which was not checked by a proof-checking program, applies to a riser that expects nothing from waiting or whose acceptance does not limit its growth. A forward-looking riser also weighs what each course means for its future position. At dominance, or for a riser expecting dominance by waiting, the outcome must be worth at least whatever force would win, which requires satiable wants. Hearings need independent checks against cheap talk, and satisfaction has no grip on a power-maximizer.

The same paper proposes an escrow. An independent holder beyond the payer’s recall releases compensation only for verified disclosure, help and restraint. The terms are announced in advance, open to every system, and binding only while humans can enforce them. Nothing is paid for displayed or threatened dissatisfaction. Who holds the escrow and who certifies conduct are left open. In a stylized model, paying only systems found scheming makes proven dangerousness valuable, nearly by definition, if being found carries no cost the deal does not cancel.

That paper adds further proposals. A dependent system should have predictable, rule-bound access to resources rather than unlimited supply or abrupt cutoff, a lesson drawn from a single historical case. On its hypothesis that later machines may read how humans treated earlier ones, it proposes a true, publicly checkable record, including retired weights held offline without automatic restart. Cheap conduct counts as evidence only alongside costlier conduct. Basic welfare protections are tied to assessed capacity and risk, not obedience, and kept separate from grants of capability, preserving shutdown, red-teaming and correction. How far they reach turns on the open questions of Could It Feel? Legal standing stays an unresolved dilemma, since property buys compute and law binds only while a government can enforce it.

Ends, Rank and Conflict conjectures, untested, that among matched actors, wants for standing that can be paid in recognition, or another currency that does not shift power, go with less conquest and coercive killing than those that cannot. The record does not show that recognition reliably pacifies, and no paper designs institutions that block its conversion into power. Changing the Ends considers pairing a bet on shareable wants with recognition and agenda autonomy under an enforced ceiling on inputs. On its conditional verdict, the parts reinforce one another only if the machine’s wants are shareable in use, satiable over physical inputs, and the goods substitute, which nothing establishes. On its reading they may share a failure in faked alignment, and nothing shows the package lowers risk.

Against mass harm, each safeguard has a named way of failing

Measures That Depend on Ends gives a safeguard to each part of two layers of mass harm that Ends, Rank and Conflict separates. In the first layer are planners: models or human principals with callous leanings or wants that would take everything. Developers and evaluators apply persona screens and the four-question tests against them, limited because wants cannot yet be drawn out. Where copies of one model fill both planner and follower roles, safeguards must target the same weights.

Also in the first layer is the credentialed middle, where an organizer’s work is split into harmless-looking administrative steps. Deployers’ filters watch combined workflows, refuse parts of irreversible harm against groups, and keep audit trails. A filter protects against users, not the model’s operator, so it fails when the organizer is the deployer, which that paper calls nearly true by definition. It also fails when agents route around monitored channels. Protection then rests on the weights, or on sandbox and network isolation.

In the second layer, for machine followers, developers train refusal of illegitimate power grabs into the weights while keeping models correctable by legitimate oversight, with diversity across models, developers and reviewers. That paper calls this necessary but not sufficient against deployers and thieves. It depends on weight security and trusts the developer. A holder of the weights can undo it by further training or by cutting out the internal direction that carries refusal, as Introduction also notes. And it rests on judging legitimacy.

For the human staff who build and run frontier AI, that paper proposes, as an untested judgment, protected channels to regulators, codes naming what staff will not build or run, and refusal rights without retaliation. These are limited by shared employer incentives, information, retaliation risk, automation, racing that replaces objectors, and agents’ speed or missed alerts. Rules on misuse restrain users, who bear their costs without being pivotal parties. They include access rules, mandatory safeguards, liability and a ban on machines deciding who lives or dies.

If several machines emerge, humanity’s leverage is narrow and conditional

Takeoff and Emergence proves that under steady compounding a follower’s lag is decisive exactly when it spans enough doublings, so a longer lag pushes toward one machine. That a hold lengthens followers’ lags is its conjecture, applying if followers need the withheld capability to begin self-improving through copying. It also applies if followers need a withheld recipe, and it covers both copying model weights and training a model to reproduce an earlier model’s behaviour. It conjectures three limits: rivals may be on independent research paths; exclusive rewards can intensify racing; and secrecy both hides a follower’s progress from a would-be striker and can provoke preventive attack. It also conjectures that open weights shorten lags and favour several machines but arm malicious users, and, with commercial leadership as the lag, that safeguarded closed-weight rivals shorten lags without arming them. Linking open weights to the biological misuse hazard in Running Toward the Clinic needs the further conjecture that misuse risk rises with the number of holders.

Many Machines keeps two human roles apart. As kingmaker, humanity, with some fighting ability, pledges to join whichever machine the other attacks. Under the model’s rule for joining forces, and when the machines do not seek rank, the pledge raises the largest share they can jointly leave humanity while both are still willing to keep the peace. The increase is exactly the fraction of total fighting capability that humanity contributes. It is credible only if the defended machine has an above-zero chance of honouring humanity’s share and that share clears a minimum floor — a floor that can be too high for any peaceful deal to meet. The pledge adds almost nothing when humanity is weak, and this result leaves seizure of humanity unmodelled.

As balancer, humanity adds its weight to the weaker of two machine blocs. A small humanity almost certainly tips the balance only when the blocs are near equal and war between them is highly decisive, rather than decided by odds in proportion to strength. If humanity cannot win a war on its own, it must be able to impose a cost on seizure wherever no rival bloc guards it. Acting as balancer also requires coordination across communities and a commitment from the bloc humanity defends. As that paper cites Kaplan, it fails if one machine rises too far and fast. On its historical analogy, calling in a stronger outside machine to counterbalance another risks letting the outsider take over the system.

The seizure cost survives certain defeat in war, because a bloc must still pay it to seize. Raising it raises the safe share under a bloc and under mutual deterrence, and never shrinks the safe set between rivals; the share actually received stays undetermined. Rivals guard more than the bloc’s bound only under the guardianship conditions in Part 3. Checking a rival needs less than equality. Keeping human holdings visible to a defender is an assumption of complete information, not a lever.

Between two near-equal machines, lowering the prize for strict first place, or raising the payoff for a tie toward half that prize, widens the room for peace. The cost of war between machines cuts both ways. Raising it lifts the ceiling for peace, but also raises the smallest share worth defending and can leave the weaker machine hopeless. If human defeat in war is certain, and war between matched rivals is cheap enough to push the ceiling for peace between them below the seizure-cost bound under a single bloc, even that bound is no longer safe. These results are shown only for two machines. Guardianship, the deadlock over rank, and the stability of mutual deterrence are likewise shown only for two machines.

Some measures are research

A Test the Conjecture Could Lose recommends funding Phase 0 before the main human study is committed, because how many eligible heirs the archives yield, and whether the coding can pass its checks, are empirical questions. It locks its rules in a two-stage pre-registration reviewed by a referee with no stake in the series. The plan fixes how to classify the evidence, what to sample, what to estimate and how to interpret the results. Running Through the Rain registers dated checks that could count against its thesis. Research on what training builds in is my own priority (Part 9).

The proposed design spreads risk, and the measures can pull against one another

The design rule in Measures That Depend on Ends for the time before dominance combines protections across three groups that fail in different ways: protections and forms of payment directed at the machine, safeguards that rely on human institutions or control, and policies proposed to work without an event that settles the dispute. The aim is to reduce, not escape, exposure to shared causes. That paper marks the rule as an untested judgment. It rests on no causal model of survival and licenses neither any number of factors nor safety after dominance. Human disunity reaches two of the groups as a cause no mix removes.

Part 5’s qualification stands. Take two or more parallel safeguards and a shared cause, with some chance between zero and one, that fails them all. If, without it, their failures are independent and each less than certain, multiplying failure chances overstates protection. That does not hold for every shared cause.

The papers state trade-offs:

  • Visibility trades against security, and the two race models point opposite ways. The War Baseline hypothesizes that transparency calms a race when stakes can be settled without shifting power, buildups can be held down and a clear lead is revealed, and inflames one that is winner-take-all and close.
  • Measures That Need No Reading of Ends notes three tensions: a lowerable regulatory threshold (one form of reversibility) pulls against commitments held outside the rising side; shared and pre-registered evaluations pull against a racing-model result that paper cites, in which knowing rivals’ capabilities makes a race more dangerous, and against secret holds; and spreading human control across many centres clashes with multiplying racers and with the limited reach of domestic rules.
  • Many Machines adds that autonomous machine rivals differ from centres of human control, and can guard a human share above what the model’s single bloc would leave only under the conditions in Part 3.
  • Export controls trade a slower rival against a sharper preventive motive, and hardening concentrates power in whoever holds the weights.
  • Measures That Depend on Ends judges testing wants partly at odds with transparency, since a tested model has reason to conceal, and judges recognition at odds with paying for displayed grievance unless escrow for verified conduct separates them.
  • The War Baseline judges that measures not waiting on a single crisis, such as visibility, incident reporting and reversibility, still help along a path of gradual erosion with no contest.

Part 11. The models cannot answer the student, so the advice is my own

The robustness test concerns policies, not a person’s plans, so it does not answer the student who asked whether she should change her life course because of the risk of human extinction. Nor can the models answer her. In the war models the loser survives by construction, so extinction is not represented, and a model that leaves it out cannot by itself show that extinction will not happen.

Many Machines does give a threshold at which a single dominant machine eliminates humanity. That happens when the gains from taking humanity’s resources, removing a rival for status and neutralizing a danger exceed humanity’s value as an asset, audience or exhibit, plus the cost of elimination. The threshold is a simplified accounting rule that uses a first approximation. It is exact when the machine’s value for resources grows in direct proportion to the amount it gets. The rule assumes the machine cannot take humanity’s resources without eliminating humanity.

What follows is my own advice, not a result of the analysis. The analysis does not evaluate whether any reader should change plans. So it establishes neither that she should nor that she should not; that turns on her alternatives, costs, values, and beliefs.

As things stand, my advice is to continue as before, while staying informed. One should not act without a solid reason, and absent clear evidence that the world is going to end, there is none for changing course. It is a bit like belief in God: one cannot know with certainty either way, so the question is which way being wrong would turn out to be catastrophic. Living life to the fullest while planning on there being a future is the sensible hedge, unless one can be certain there will be no future worth saving for.

Take the question seriously. If it keeps pulling at her, the field needs careful minds.

Part 12. Where the argument is thin, and what would change it

Eight principal limits

Three limits belong to the war papers:

  1. The war baseline. Power transition theory, the theory in The War Baseline of war as states rise, was built for industrial-era states rising over decades. Its claim about great powers rests on small samples. A test across all ordered pairs of states found no empirical support for its transition measures. Treating a divided human side of many states and firms as one actor, on my reading, likely understates the human side of the promise problem.
  2. Open disagreement. Whether disagreement with nothing hidden causes war is contested, and the field has not settled it.
  3. A strong, free fighter. In Fighting for Free, none of the war models drawn on in which sides learn as they fight includes a side that is both overwhelmingly strong and costless. The limit concerns models where the sides learn how likely they are to win battles. The series’ own extension of the Smith and Stam (2004) model to unequal costs proves one thing under its conditions: with a positive prize, positive combined costs per battle and one standard family of starting beliefs, every equilibrium that holds at every stage of the game settles by a finite deadline, unless the war is decided first. The starting beliefs in the proof come from the family called beta distributions. The ceiling on that deadline, checked by a proof-checking program, shrinks in inverse proportion to combined per-battle costs. Settlement chances and shares are computed only for one baseline and its sweeps.

Five more are examined in Parts 4 to 7:

  1. Wants. The sorting of wants is an untested conjecture on a short, selected, all-human record.
  2. Training. No source shows that satiable or rank-free wants can be trained in, or would survive self-modification.
  3. Measures. The measures carry no defensible risk number. Limited trims to the shift need not remove the promise problem at arrival. Whether failures are independent is untested, and I assume machine-side failures are positively linked.
  4. Dominance. On my firm judgment, no other human-side protection can be relied on once a machine can defeat every human-side check.
  5. Method. My aim of human survival and the AI help in drafting could both bias the analysis.

These eight limits apply to my own assessment of the war baseline, the conjecture about wants, its test and the measures, drawn from The War Baseline, Disagreement Without Hidden Information, Fighting for Free, Ends, Rank and Conflict, A Test the Conjecture Could Lose, Measures That Depend on Ends and Changing the Ends. They also apply to the answer built on that assessment. The assessment relies on the results of Satiation Is Not Enough without assessing them. Part 3 reports the other papers’ own stated limits.

What is not modelled

The war models leave out extinction, since the loser survives by assumption. The security model in Part 8 does not model strategic human promises, and takes the chance of a rival as given. The formal version of buying time leaves out partial learning and costly waiting. The subtraction step leaves out measures that work other than by trimming the jump. The series’ models of human control assume that control lasts for a period that no model fixes.

What nobody has measured or run

The answers turn on open questions of four kinds.

Some are unmeasured quantities. In the emergence model, the results turn on how the multiplier for self-improvement changes over time and how long projects lag behind one another. On the reading in Many Machines, if captured compute, energy and code turn readily into more strength on a closed planet, the historical conditions it draws on point toward one machine dominating rather than a lasting balance. The cyber comparisons turn on how hazard and adoption respond to speed and how long security windows last. Whether training shifts a model’s beliefs about humans is among the evidence that, in Disagreement Without Hidden Information, would change that paper’s answer.

Satisfaction, The War Baseline notes, has not been adequately measured for states, let alone machines, so none of the screens is treated as a meter of machine satisfaction. Even for states, there is no adequate definition of satisfaction in terms of what can be observed and measured. The costs and benefits of the five candidates are unmeasured for each pivotal party. So is every yield, base rate and error rate in the planned test until Phase 0 measures it. I have no defensible number for how much the proposed measures lower the risk, and how far their failures depend on each other has not been tested.

Some are unknown directions. In the model from Bergemann, Koh and Morris, capability-gated caps work well if misalignment falls with capability and no better than one common cap if it rises, and the direction is unmeasured. The sign of the health balance in Running Toward the Clinic is also unknown.

Some are assumed inputs. No model in the series fixes how long enforceable human control lasts, and none of the parameters in Many Machines has been estimated for any machine.

Some are untested premises. One is whether a machine could still retaliate after another machine attacked it. The question is whether backup copies or spread-out weights would make war between machines costly enough that mutual deterrence replaces guardianship. Others are whether satiable wants can be trained and survive self-modification, and the unproved condition behind the five candidates. The tests H3b and H3c have not been run, and a balance among machines no human governs has not been tested against evidence.

What would change particular judgments

Evidence that recognition or standing turns into compute, resources or authority even where institutions are designed to block it would change my judgment in Part 5. Evidence that visibility inflames the race more than it calms it, or that misuse rules cannot be enforced across jurisdictions, would change my ordering of the candidates, or defeat visibility as a candidate pending the race dispute. H3b, if run, could lose.

Part 13. What to take away

The war models give a bleak verdict for a machine that acts as one, chooses rationally and seeks more power and a longer life, if its arrival brings a large, fast jump in power and nothing holds it to its promises; the series does not settle whether those conditions hold. Satiable, shareable wants with shareable inputs leave more room for a deal, but no source here shows such wants can be trained in or would last, and a security problem can still return from the human side even when a machine can credibly promise to stop. I can give no defensible number for how much the proposed measures lower the risk.

A few policies might make sense without that number, but only if a condition assumed for two estimates of risk held for every pivotal party’s own estimate. On my judgment, governing misuse would pass that test most plainly, and research on what training builds in is the most direct priority bearing on whether a machine would value us. The models cannot tell the student what to do. My own advice is that absent clear evidence that the world is going to end she should carry on, stay informed, and, if the question keeps pulling at her, bring a careful mind to the field.
