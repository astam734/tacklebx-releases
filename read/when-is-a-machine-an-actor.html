In brief. In my model, a machine can pass the test defined here for having coherent ends, the premise war-bargaining models start from, without anyone first settling whether it is conscious, for theories of consciousness that look only at how a machine is built. That holds because of how the test is defined, not because of anything discovered about machines. For a machine that makes one fixed choice in each situation, the test asks whether one single ranking of outcomes puts every chosen outcome strictly above the other options across the situations considered. Working software need not pass it. Under stated conditions, selection in the model can favour a policy that maximises payoff and passes the ends test on the situations used to score it. By convention, copies of one version count as one actor over a set of situations only when they cover that set between them and their pooled choices contain no circle, and every retrained version is a new actor. Whether a machine can feel, whether it lives, and whether its ends are truly its own all stay open.

Part 1. Before asking how a machine would bargain, we must ask when it counts as one actor

For decades the study of international conflict has modelled states as single decision-makers that choose the best means to their ends, given what they believe. States, however, are not machines. To ask what a highly capable artificial system would do in bargaining or war, we first have to ask when its conduct justifies treating it as a strategic actor at all. We also have to ask where that actor’s edges lie, since software can be copied at will and retrained into new versions.

Here, as part of a series on capable machines, I take up five questions that sit at the turn from treating machines as tools to modelling them as strategic actors:

  1. Does passing my test for having ends wait on whether the machine is conscious?
  2. In what thin sense is a machine “rational,” and why model it that way when software and human states are built so differently?
  3. What does selection among deployed models fix about what a machine wants, and what does it leave open?
  4. When do many running copies of one model count as a single actor?
  5. When does a retrained version become a new actor?

A few plain terms will carry us through. A situation is the setting in which one choice is made, including temporary working state such as what the machine currently believes. The technical paper calls it a “context.” A menu is the set of outcomes available in a situation. A machine’s policy is its rule for what to do in each situation. Its build is the set of architectural features that theories of consciousness look at; the technical paper calls these its “structural coordinates.”

What a machine wants, its ends, is defined here by a single test. I call it the ends test: a machine passes on a set of situations if one ranking of outcomes explains every choice it makes there. The ranking must put the chosen outcome strictly above every other option on that situation’s menu. The technical paper calls this “strict rationalisability” and calls such a ranking a “rationalising witness.” The test covers only machines that make one fixed choice in each situation, not ones that choose at random.

The short answers run as follows. In the model, passing the ends test does not wait on consciousness for theories of consciousness that look only at a machine’s build. Being rational in this thin sense is neither guaranteed by working code nor fixed everywhere by selection. By convention, copies of one version count as one actor over a set of situations only when they meet every situation in that set between them and their pooled choices never run in a circle, and each new version is a new actor.

Many of the formal results are checked by a proof-checking program (Lean 4). Such a program confirms only that a conclusion follows from its stated assumptions. I report these checks from a proof project, and the draft of the technical paper supplies no link to where that project is deposited. Some results are checked only under narrower conditions than the plain statement suggests, and a few clauses are not established at all (Part 10).

I take several inputs as given from neighbouring papers in the series. The War Baseline, the series paper on when the bargaining model of war applies to a capable machine, supplies four baseline conditions. Could It Feel?, the series paper on machine consciousness, supplies the description of a machine’s build, a roster of theories of consciousness, a measure of how well a build persists, and a basic theorem about selection on build features. In turn, I hand the bounded actor rule set out here to Ends, Rank and Conflict, which varies what the actor wants, and to Measures That Depend on Ends, which examines governance measures and moral protections; Changing the Ends takes up whether copies would share a goal closely enough to sacrifice for one another.

What here is standard, and what is new? Standard are the single rational actor of conflict studies (Allison 1969; Bendor and Hammond 1992; Bennett and Stam 2004), the equation for how the shares of competing groups change with their relative success (Harper 2009), the internal penalty for withholding cooperation in a game played once (Frank 2008), the benchmark for many units becoming one (Szathmáry 2015), and the philosophical, cognitive-science and AI-safety work surveyed below. I define the ends test so that beliefs and other temporary working state are part of the situation. The test reads only the policy, so it can be applied independently of theories of consciousness that read only the build. Part 3 treats the sameness of the stable patterns of play across consciousness theories as bookkeeping when the game and beliefs are fixed and payoffs ignore the theories’ verdicts. It also gives a separate check of the penalty in a game played once. Part 6 applies the selection model to policies, and Parts 7 and 8 set out the conventions for copies and versions.

Part 2. The war baseline assumes one consistent decision-maker, and nothing in its list mentions consciousness

Most work in the rationalist tradition assumes that states are unitary and rational. Unitary means each acts as a single decision-maker; rational means it chooses the best means to its ends given its beliefs. Realism adds that the ends are power, or what power secures. Some research programmes narrow the leader’s aim: selectorate theory models leaders who seek above all to keep office (Bueno de Mesquita et al. 2003).

Drawing on an earlier statement of that programme, Alastair Smith and I distinguished leaders who care about policy, who may settle disputes more easily, from leaders after “booty” (Smith and Stam 2001). D. Scott Bennett and I argued that war decisions made by “a single leader” or “a small committee” fit rational choice well (Bennett and Stam 2004). We found little consistent evidence, though, that our game fit better as the stakes rose.

The War Baseline rests its machine version of this model on four conditions:

  1. The machine is unitary and rational: its choices fit one set of ends across situations.
  2. It seeks to maximise its power and its duration. For a copyable machine, duration means its trained numerical settings (“weights”) and goal persisting across copies.
  3. Once it can protect itself, and the contested resources cannot be badly damaged by humans, fighting costs it far less than it costs human states, measured as a share of the prize. That cost measure includes wartime destruction of the contested resources.
  4. What it wants, computing power, energy and matter, can be divided, transferred and accumulated.

Ends, Rank and Conflict adds a rule for attributing specific ends beyond that baseline. It ascribes an “end-like disposition” only when the machine pursues it stably across situations and copies, judged by the copies test of Part 7.

Together these assumptions describe what Allison called the Model I actor: a decision-maker with “one set of specified goals (the equivalent of a consistent utility function)” (Allison 1969). Model I is a lens the analyst adopts. It needs no evidence of inner experience, and nothing in the war baseline’s list mentions consciousness. The ends test checks the single-ranking part of this picture. It leaves out how beliefs enter the weighing of risks and the rest of the bargaining machinery.

The evidence the study of war draws on, however, comes from human-staffed states. The historical evidence on competition for rank and standing in Ends, Rank and Conflict concerns the human leaders who govern those states. When The War Baseline turns survival into the persistence of weights and goal, the strategic end carries over but the biological or institutional mechanism does not. Two questions must therefore be kept apart. The first is whether the formal rational-actor model is well defined without any reference to consciousness, and it is. The second is whether the historical record of conflict transfers to a machine. That record comes from conscious human decision-makers, so consciousness is among the undeclared differences.

Part 3. In my judgment, consciousness gates some questions, but not the mathematics of the strategic model

Consciousness, or a neighbouring biological property, might be proposed as a gate on treating a machine as an actor in five different ways. I take each in turn. Each verdict is my judgment from the evidence surveyed, not a proof.

Is consciousness needed for having ends at all? The literature is split

The first reading makes consciousness a gate for agency and ends themselves. One camp detaches agency from experience. List (2018) defines agents by beliefs and desires, so that states and corporations qualify. If a theory known as integrated information theory is right, he tentatively argues that such organisations have a kind of awareness that shows up in how they function while lacking felt experience of their own. Butlin (2024) argues that reinforcement learners (systems trained by reward and penalty) meet the conditions for minimal agency. He also argues that learners of this kind that use a model of their environment can act for reasons without consciously reflecting on them. Block concludes that “future AI systems could conceivably have agency without consciousness” (Block 2026). Evolutionary models by Alger and Weibull (2013) produce stable ends with no appeal to experience, though applying them to machine weights or copies is a further step. In their models, whether the ends are stable depends on who interacts with whom.

Two dissenting traditions stand on the other side. Christine Korsgaard holds that acting for a reason requires awareness of one’s motive as fitting. A lioness lacks that, though “she is an agent, not a mechanism” (Korsgaard 2008). For something to be good for the machine itself, Korsgaard requires experience with a felt good-or-bad quality. In her words, the condition is that it “was conscious and had valenced experiences that guided her to pursue her own functional good” (Korsgaard 2018). She also rejects reading a self-conscious agent’s ends off its conduct. If you do, she objects, you could never catch that agent failing to take the means to its end.

John Searle’s Chinese room argument denies that symbol manipulation yields mental “aboutness” (what philosophers call intentionality), and with it genuine ends: “Any mechanism capable of producing intentionality must have causal powers equal to those of the brain” (Searle 1980). Chalmers (1995) contests the parallel claim for experience. Panksepp (2011) holds that primal feelings shape choice by signalling whether an action supports survival. By contrast, Seth (2025) distinguishes kinds of agency rather than gating it on consciousness. Godfrey-Smith’s (2020) link between animal agency and subjectivity bites only for systems with brain-like biology.

The state analogy has a limit here. Conscious human officials form and carry out a state’s ends, and List’s group agents also have conscious members. These examples show that ends can be attributed to an organisation that may itself lack experience. They do not show ends in a system with no experience anywhere inside it.

For the war baseline, the conclusion is narrow. Model I needs no evidence of experience, so these disputes bear on whether a real machine fits the frame, not on whether the frame is valid. For self-conscious agents, Korsgaard argues that stable ends require the agent to examine and endorse its own reasons for acting. For animals she credits unity to instinct. Her argument therefore does not reach a machine without reflective self-consciousness. On my reading, though, her texts grant such a machine no other source of unity either. For a reflectively self-conscious machine her objection applies, and the analysis becomes the conditional one described in Part 5. My verdict: consciousness is not a gate for the mathematics of the baseline, but whether a real machine’s ends are genuinely its own remains in dispute.

Is life, rather than consciousness, the gate? On one tradition, yes

The second reading puts the gate at biological life. Fleming and Shea (2025) write that “having a genuine goal may furthermore require having genuine needs, perhaps even metabolism.” Thompson’s (2004) enactive account gives real purposes only to living, self-producing systems, conscious or not.

If that tradition is right, the question is whether a machine actively maintains its own existence. Could It Feel? models that as a property of the build, through its measure of persistence and the regulation of a variable the system depends on. It also models pressure to retain signals that mark conditions as good or bad for the system. The strategic mathematics is untouched, because it reads only the policy. Yet on the enactive view a non-living silicon system has no ends of its own, so applying an ends-based model to it is the very step in dispute. My verdict: within that tradition, life or self-maintenance gates the ascription of a machine’s own ends, and the gate is life, not consciousness.

Does moral status turn on consciousness? On one view

The third reading makes consciousness a gate for moral status, which is where the literature puts the most weight on experience. List (2018) suggests, as a position one “could argue,” that the capacity for felt inner experience (“phenomenal consciousness”) is a necessary condition for moral significance in its own right. Long, Sebo and colleagues (2024) hold open another route to moral standing through agency. Both works are parties to the live debate over AI welfare. Measures That Depend on Ends adopts a practical rule: treat moral status as an open question in public, and keep basic protections independent of obedience.

If a machine is conscious, that may change what others owe it. It may also add a new kind of end: its own welfare. Could It Feel? models that welfare objective. Chandaria and colleagues (2026), an AI-developer-affiliated work and itself a party to the debate, note that one deployed model may be “potentially many” subjects that can be harmed, sometimes called moral patients, if its instances or threads are conscious.

Who acts and how many can be harmed are separate questions. The copies rule of Part 7 fixes the unit of strategic choice. It does not fix the count of moral subjects, which stays open for the consciousness analysis of Could It Feel? and the governance measures of Measures That Depend on Ends. My verdict: on one philosophical view consciousness gates moral status, and the strategic actor stays separate from the count of moral patients.

Does the analogy with human geniuses need felt enjoyment? Partly

The fourth reading concerns an analogy in Ends, Rank and Conflict. That paper asks whether a first-rank mind might care more about understanding the world than about dominating rivals, drawing on the lives of great scientists. Part of that evidence is outward conduct, but part is first-person reports of feeling. There, the condition under which the drive to know beats rank-seeking is that the pursuit is enjoyed from within. Among that analogy’s deepest limits are motives a machine may lack, including fellow feeling and being sometimes satisfied.

The analogy carries over only if a machine has something that does the same job as inner enjoyment. No formal model in my sources separates that role from the felt quality. Frank (2008), for example, calls his penalty term “a proxy for guilt feelings.” Thinkers who define mental states by the working role they play (“functionalists”) would say the role is all that matters; Panksepp would say the feeling is the role. No source I read resolves this for intellectual enjoyment. Korsgaard (2018) treats science itself as one of the specifically human, rational modes of knowing, which on her view requires reflection. Whether a machine seeks knowledge as an end of its own depends on the standard used. The standard might require reflection on its motives, experience that feels good or bad, biological self-maintenance, or simply stable pursuit across situations. Could It Feel? examines when selection keeps a signal that marks conditions as good or bad for the system.

My verdict: consciousness is not a gate under the behavioural ends test. But felt enjoyment is an uncontrolled difference in the historical evidence, and should be declared alongside embodiment, mortality and biological evolution.

Does consciousness change anything in strategy? The same stable patterns of play hold when the game and beliefs are fixed and payoffs ignore the verdict

The fifth reading says consciousness is no gate anywhere. Put that broadly, the claim is too strong, both for the reasons above and because consciousness changes what human conduct toward machines signals (Dreksler et al. 2025).

When the game and beliefs are fixed and the payoffs ignore the theories’ verdicts about consciousness, result L4 gives the same set of equilibria under every theory. An equilibrium is a pattern of play in which no player gains by changing its strategy alone. The set stays the same by bookkeeping because the game itself stays the same.

Take the drive to survive. Turner and colleagues study agents that maximise average reward. They prove that for most ways of assigning rewards, “average-optimal agents would generally stop us from deactivating them” (Turner et al. 2021). That theorem makes no appeal to consciousness, but it covers only optimal policies. Whether trained systems in open environments share the drive is contested. Omohundro (2008) and Hendrycks (2023) argue that self-preservation emerges in capable systems anyway. Only accounts of bodily self-regulation tie feeling to self-preservation, and only for self-regulating systems (Solms 2019; Seth and Tsakiris 2018). Conversely, a conjecture drawn from Hinton (2022) and Aru and colleagues (2023) holds that backups may lower the value any one instance puts on its own survival, conscious or not.

Now consider what can make a commitment credible. Frank (2008) uses a game played once in which both players do better if both cooperate than if neither does, but each can gain by withholding cooperation if the other cooperates. An internal penalty weighs against withholding cooperation. Three things must be kept apart:

  1. The penalty can change the committed player’s own best move in the one-shot game. Lemma L4 checks this.
  2. Changing an opponent’s move requires the opponent to observe or infer the penalty. L4 does not model this.
  3. Frank’s channel through impulse control belongs to repeated play, which is not modelled here.

Consciousness could enter strategy through three channels. One is a welfare term in the actor’s aims, as in Could It Feel?. Another is what human conduct signals and what others owe a conscious machine. The third is the internal penalty in the commitment example. The war baseline uses none of the three. My sources leave open whether felt experience sets that penalty (in the one-shot move, an observable commitment signal if one could be verified, or repeated-play self-control), and whether an emotional-commitment term in The War Baseline’s commitment analysis requires real feeling or only a state that plays the same role in choice. This is where the evidence is thinnest.

My verdict: in a fixed game with payoffs that ignore consciousness verdicts and fixed beliefs, stable outcomes are unchanged across theories of consciousness. An internal penalty can change the committed player’s own best move in a one-shot game. Any effect on an opponent through observing or inferring it lies outside L4.

Part 4. Working code need not choose consistently, and on my reading training pushes behaviour toward solving a decision problem

In the war literature, a rational side chooses consistently with one set of ends given its beliefs. Neither that notion nor the ends test means wise or benign, and neither says what the ends are. The War Baseline fixes the ends at power and duration; Ends, Rank and Conflict varies them. Treating a machine as rational still needs defending.

Coherent code. A program built from flawless code can still choose inconsistently. A trained system’s choices are learned, not hand-written. In a small checked example, the rule picks the first of two outcomes in one situation and the second in another situation that offers both. No single ranking of the outcomes can put each choice ahead of the other.

Optimisation. What links frontier machines to rational choice is how they are made. Training searches for behaviour that scores well, and systems built to act are trained to maximise their average reward across the outcomes they might face, weighted by how likely those outcomes are, which is the problem rational-choice theory states. Hubinger and colleagues (2019) argue that training can also produce a model that itself searches for good actions, under an aim that need not match the one it was trained on.

On my reading, training pushes behaviour toward solving a decision problem. Turner and colleagues warn, however, that optimal policies are “often qualitatively divorced from the actual policies learned by reinforcement learning” (Turner et al. 2021, quoted from the 2023 revision). Reward shapes a policy; it is not automatically that policy’s goal. The War Baseline therefore models a system that solves its decision problem, which today’s systems do not. That rising capability brings learned behaviour closer to that solution is my conjecture, not a finding.

That argument concerns a single agent. The bargaining models of The War Baseline and Disagreement Without Hidden Information, the series paper on disagreement in bargaining, ask more: each side plays its best response to the other’s strategy, and each knows the other is rational. Another small proof check shows that training against a fixed environment need not produce that kind of play against a strategic opponent.

Capability. In my judgment, a superintelligence would loosen limits on computing a best choice more than any human state could, though not every limit. It would still face rivals as capable as itself and problems that are inherently hard to compute. A model trained on human writing may also inherit human framing effects.

Even with looser computing limits, carrying the rational-actor assumption from human leaders to a machine remains a conditional conjecture whose support comes from selection in a formal model rather than fit to the historical record. Bennett and I found that rational choice fits decisions by “a single leader” or “a small committee.” I argue that the selection results of Part 6 support carrying this over to a machine only under their stated conditions on the tested situations, while consistency across tested and untested situations together and unity across copies (Part 7) remain separate premises.

Pressure toward consistency. An agent whose strict preferences over goods run in a circle, who prefers more money with any given good, and who will pay a positive fee for each swap it strictly prefers can be milked for money: a rival can offer a series of swaps the agent prefers, bringing it back to its starting good with less money than before. This “money pump” is checked in a minimal model. Omohundro (2008) argues on these grounds that capable self-improving systems will act to make themselves rational. Under the version rule of Part 8, though, any self-improvement that changes the trained weights makes a new actor.

Two limits apply, both checked in minimal models. First, any pattern of choice, even a circular one, is explained by some ranking if the ranking may change with the situation, menu or history of each choice. Pressure toward consistency therefore shapes the form of a machine’s preferences across situations, not the content of its ends. Second, the money pump needs someone to offer the trades. In my judgment that pressure bears on settings with several machines, as in Many Machines, the series paper on interaction among machines, not on one machine acting alone.

There is early evidence on today’s systems. Comparing roughly two dozen chat models, Mazeika and colleagues (2025) find that forced choices over 500 hypothetical outcomes show fewer circular patterns in models that score higher on a standard knowledge benchmark. A single scoring rule for outcomes also fits the choices of the higher-scoring models better. They conclude that “as LLMs grow in scale, their preferences become more coherent and well-represented by utilities” (Mazeika et al. 2025). The comparison is across different models at one time, the preferences are stated answers to hypothetical text choices, and the study is a preprint.

Part 5. In the model, what a machine wants is read off its choices, by definition

To make the split between strategy and consciousness exact, the model describes a machine in two parts. Its build is the architectural features on which the theories in Could It Feel? are defined. Its policy is a rule giving one fixed action in each of a finite set of situations, with each action producing an outcome. The model has finitely many actions and outcomes. The action and the situation together specify which outcome results. Because the policy gives one choice per situation, randomly sampled choices and probability mixes over actions (“mixing”) fall outside the test (Part 10).

By my analytical decision, ends are defined on the policy alone. A machine has ends on a set of situations if some ranking of outcomes puts its chosen outcome strictly above every other option on each menu. With finitely many outcomes, this is the same as saying its choices contain no circle. For example, a policy fails if it picks the first outcome over the second, the second over the third, and the third over the first when offered each pair.

Beliefs sit inside the situation. The situations compared may include different beliefs; holding beliefs fixed restricts which situations are compared. By this definition, a machine that updates a belief and then reverses its choice over the same menu fails the test, even if it chose well given what it believed. So bargaining under private information needs a separate account of beliefs and updating. The test also says nothing about choices among gambles or how beliefs about the chances of possible outcomes affect how a machine weighs those outcomes. Because the ranking must be strict, a failure under exact payoff ties reflects that strictness, not a failure to do the best available.

Three features of the definition matter.

  1. The test bites only where menus overlap. If no two situations share an outcome, no circle can form and every policy passes, trivially.
  2. Internal states go into the situation, not the outcome. Features of the outside world that matter to payoff can be part of an outcome’s description. But anything held only in runtime state, such as a belief in the active text window (“context window”), temporary working memory (a “cache”), or a copy’s assigned role, enters only the situation. So a reversal driven by such state counts as failing the test and the copies test, even when the machine chooses well or copies are dividing labour. That follows from the definition; it is not a misreading. The other convention would make menus in situations with different states disjoint, removing the test’s bite across states. Under that other convention, the copies test of Part 7 would still require that copies meet all the situations considered and pick the same outcome whenever they share a situation. Under my convention, Part 6’s consistency result still holds for a policy that picks the highest-valued outcome under a valuation that depends only on outcomes and has no ties within any tested menu. Such a policy picks the same outcome whenever it faces the same menu. The rule builds on discussion of state-dependent choice in Chandaria and colleagues (2026), and says nothing about what internal states any named system has.
  3. The ends test can be applied independently of consciousness because it reads only the policy. In a real machine, policy and build can share the same hardware; the formal split concerns which parts each test reads. The ends test reads only the policy. So, in the model, having ends does not depend on consciousness for any theory that reads only the build, like those in Could It Feel?.

The welfare objective of Could It Feel?, which adds a welfare term across subjects, is used by no result here. It assumes a numerical scale on which welfare can be measured and added across subjects.

When a machine passes, what do its choices reveal about the ranking? Lemma L6 answers. With finitely many outcomes, the choices fix which of two distinct outcomes ranks higher exactly when a chain of observed choices leads from one to the other. For example, if the machine chooses the first over the second in one situation and the second over the third elsewhere, the ranking must put the first above the third. The technical paper calls this the “transitive closure” of revealed preference. Two further clauses, about rankings off those chains and about measuring strength of preference, are not established.

Two dissents stand against reading ends off conduct: the enactive view that genuine ends need biological self-production (Thompson 2004; Fleming and Shea 2025), and Korsgaard’s account (Korsgaard 2008, 2018). I adopt neither in the formal definition. Korsgaard’s requirement for a good of one’s own does, though, agree with confining the welfare term to machines that can feel. Acting for a reason has a different requirement in her account. The agent reflects on and endorses its motives, then chooses both what to aim for and what to do. She distinguishes this from intelligence directed at the outside world and from the instinctive agency of animals. Her texts separate ideas that discussions of AI often blur:

What is attributed What Korsgaard requires
Agency at all A conscious organism guided by felt good-or-bad representations; no reflection needed
Purposes of one’s own Nothing beyond animal agency
A good of one’s own Consciousness of a felt good-or-bad kind
Acting for a reason, choosing ends, autonomy Reflecting on and endorsing one’s motives as reasons to act
Moral agency The same reflective self-awareness

Her challenge has two prongs. Against reading a reflective agent’s ends off conduct, she objects that such an agent could then never fail to take the means to its end. She aims this at reflective agents and grants animals unity through instinct, though on my reading her texts give a non-reflective machine no other source of unity either. Against giving a machine without felt experience a good of its own, her dissent holds outright. Readers who share her view, or the enactive one, can read The War Baseline and Ends, Rank and Conflict as conditional. They say what follows if a machine shows stable, end-like conduct, without granting that its ends are reflectively willed or biologically self-produced.

Part 6. In the model, selection backs consistent choices only where it tested them, and only under stated conditions

Why should a machine’s policy pass the ends test anywhere, and how far does consistency reach? I answer with a model of selection among deployed lineages. A lineage is the chain of versions of a model. By convention, the unit of selection in this model is the lineage copied through deployment. As an analogy only: think of a breeder who keeps and multiplies whichever animals do best at the tasks the breeder sets, and never sees how they would handle tasks never set.

In the model, a lineage’s share grows with its fitness. Fitness adds up the benefits it earns, including its task payoff, and subtracts its costs. Each benefit is given a weight before it is added. Fitness also includes a term based on how the consciousness theory evaluates the build. The term gets zero weight on the view that consciousness has no direct effect on fitness (Robinson 2023) and positive weight on the view that it can have an effect (James 1879). The total is scaled by how well the lineage’s build persists.

The model is simplified. Harper (2009) links this growth rule to probability-based belief updating (“Bayesian updating”), but no result cited carries it to step-by-step weight adjustment (“gradient-based training”) within one run. The model assumes a fixed environment, whereas real fitness depends on developers and markets and can shift in response. It also ignores chance among finitely many lineages.

The basic theorem in Could It Feel? concerns build features. When two builds differ in one feature, that feature wins out when its benefit, plus any direct gain from consciousness, beats its extra cost at equal persistence. This assumes no mutation, meaning each type produces copies of its own type only. The benefit in that condition is the weighted total across the pressures acting on the lineage. Theorem R3 turns that model from build to policy.

R3 needs some set-up. Each situation carries a weight in selection. Across the finitely many situations, those weights are zero or positive, never negative. The tested situations are those with positive weight; the technical paper calls them “tested contexts.” Task payoff is scored by a valuation that depends on the outcome alone, not on the situation. Several things are assumed to depend only on the build: benefits other than task payoff, energy cost and persistence. Total cost depends only on the build and on choices in tested situations. Outside the tested situations, the policy touches fitness only through task payoff, which carries zero weight there. If actions changed persistence directly, the cancellations below would break.

R3 compares a payoff-maximising policy, one that picks the best-valued outcome in every tested situation, with a rival of the same build. These are distinct policy types. If they were realised by different trained weights, the version rule of Part 8 would count them as different actors, though R3 neither compares successive versions nor maps policies to weights. R3 has four clauses.

R3(a): the maximiser is consistent on the tested situations. Suppose the valuation has no ties within any menu on the tested situations. Then the payoff-maximising policy passes the ends test there, and the ranking that explains it is the one used to score outcomes for selection. This follows at once from picking the best each time. But because the explaining ranking is the selector’s, R3(a) cannot by itself tell a machine with ends of its own from one faithfully carrying out its selector’s valuation. Finer descriptions of outcomes can make menus disjoint and weaken the test. A reward that cannot be written as one valuation of outcomes, as when the prompt changes the value of the same outcome, falls outside R3(a). With ties, R3(a) can fail.

R3(b): when the maximiser wins. On the tested situations the maximiser earns at least as much task payoff as any same-build rival. Because the two share a build, they have the same persistence, other benefits and consciousness value, so those terms cancel out under every theory of consciousness. This holds whether a theory gives a yes-or-no verdict or a degree. R3(b) compares two types in a fixed environment, with both present at the start. The result also assumes the model’s restrictions on its parameters and the bound on fitness hold.

  • Without mutation, the maximiser takes over from every mixed starting share if and only if its weighted payoff gain on the tested situations exceeds its extra cost. This holds for every theory of consciousness, every weight on consciousness, and every cost that depends only on the build and on choices in tested situations.
  • Without mutation, if its extra cost exceeds that gain, it does not take over, and the rival, consistent or not, takes over instead.
  • With a positive mutation rate below one half, running equally both ways, the same condition holds if and only if the maximiser holds more than half the population at the model’s rest point with both types present. If the maximiser’s weighted payoff gain exceeds its extra cost, the rival’s share at that point is capped in proportion to the mutation rate.
  • With more than two types present, beating one rival does not establish winning against all.

Under the same without-mutation takeover assumptions, that cancellation also covers comparing a machine before and after training on its policy alone, leaving its build fixed. Training that changes the build falls under the basic theorem of Could It Feel? instead.

R3(c): consistency is not rewarded for its own sake. Selection rewards gross task payoff, not consistency as such. A circular policy can out-earn a consistent one. Take the three two-option menus from Part 5, weighted equally. The first offers outcomes worth 3 and 2, the second offers 2 and 1, and the third offers 1 and 3. The circular policy earns 3, 2 and 1 from those menus, averaging 2, and fails the ends test. A consistent policy that ranks outcomes in reverse order of value earns 2, 1 and 1, averaging one and a third. The consistent policy that ranks outcomes by value earns 3, 2 and 3, the highest average of two and two-thirds.

R3(d): untested behaviour is not constrained by selection in the model. Two points must be kept apart. First, under the fitness assumptions above, any two policies with the same build that agree on the tested situations have identical fitness, because nothing in fitness reads the untested ones. That equality holds even if the model restricts which policies are allowed. Second, the freedom to vary untested choices independently comes from letting the policy be any table at all from situations to actions. In real networks, shared parameters and a built-in bias toward simple functions tie tested and untested behaviour together. Those are outside the model. And by Lemma L6, with finitely many outcomes, even consistent tested choices fix the strict ranking of two distinct outcomes only along chains of observed choices.

When does selection reach beyond the tested set? A cost that depends on untested choices can break R3(d)’s equal-fitness result if it charges different amounts for otherwise equally fit policies. A cost on description length is an example: it can charge differently for untested choices and so separate policies that agree where tested. When Ends, Rank and Conflict analyses a superintelligence’s ends beyond its training, it takes R3(d) and Lemma L6 from here and must state which cost assumption it adopts.

R3 also clarifies what evidence on current language models shows. Modelling preferences needs only what Chalmers (2026) calls a “quasi-subject” whose behaviour reads as beliefs and desires; such working states “are cheap.” I take no position on whether a coherent subject stands behind a model’s persona. In forced binary choices, Mazeika and colleagues (2025) find circular patterns “below 1% for the largest LLMs,” while noting that “it remains unclear whether the resulting preferences are coherent or merely random arrangements.” When Khan and colleagues (2025) allowed a neutral option, preferences proved frame-dependent: GPT-4o took it “in 100% of country pair comparisons” (Khan et al. 2025).

My conjecture from R3 holds under the model’s assumptions only. Under the conditions of R3(a) and R3(b), a selected lineage should pass consistency tests on its tested set. By R3(d), under the stated fitness assumptions, same-build policies that agree on tested choices are equally fit. The unrestricted policy table lets their untested choices vary independently of their tested choices. A cost that reads untested choices can make those policies differ in fitness. I give no method for defining situations, outcomes, menus or the tested set for a real system.

Part 7. By convention, copies of one version count as one actor only when they cover the situations and their pooled choices contain no circle

A human leader cannot be duplicated overnight into a million identical decision-makers. A set of trained weights can. Any strategic analysis of AI must therefore say who the actor is when a model is copied.

Allison’s (1969) three lenses (the single rational actor, organisational routines, and bureaucratic politics) suggest three candidate units:

  1. One version of a model, treating all its copies as one actor over a set of situations only when the copies test below holds there. This is a manageable Model I assumption, and the war baseline uses it.
  2. Each running instance or conversation thread. Chalmers (2026) takes the immediate partner in an AI conversation to be a “virtual instance” or a “thread,” which would make the count of actors enormous.
  3. The developer and model together, an organisational actor closer to Allison’s other two lenses.

By analytical convention I adopt the first. It keeps the baseline in Allison’s manageable frame, while the lineage is the unit of selection in Part 6. The third option is set aside by assumption, because it needs an organisational and bureaucratic model not built here. Which later results would survive under it is not examined. Where the copies test fails, I do not say what the right unit is. Again, this fixes the strategic unit, not the number of moral subjects, which stays open between model, instance and thread.

Why is unity across copies an assumption rather than automatic? Weights copy exactly. But each running instance has its own context window and cache, and differences in what they hold put copies in different situations. Copies run on parallel hardware using operations that can return different results from one run to the next can also drift apart in their outputs (Shanmugavelu et al. 2024). Such drift falls outside the same-policy simplification of the copies test below.

Political science has argued over unity before. Allison (1969) criticised treating states as “black boxes covering various gears and levers.” Bendor and Hammond (1992) replied that the unitary assumption exists “to conserve the intellectual resources of scholars.” In biology, Szathmáry (2015) sets the benchmark for many units becoming one organism: “a high level of cooperation and a low level of realized conflicts.” Identical copies start with no reproductive conflict. Whether diverging contexts create working conflict, the behavioural check below does not measure.

Lemma L5 defines when copies are unitary over a target set of situations. Each copy runs its own fixed-choice policy on the situations it actually meets. Two conditions must both hold:

  1. Coverage. Between them, the copies meet every situation in the target set. This fails whenever some situation is met by no copy.
  2. Pooled consistency. Pool all the copies’ choices together; the pooled record contains no circle.

Notice what L5 certifies and what it does not. It certifies that pooled choice is consistent over the situations covered. It does not test whether copies share an inner goal or would sacrifice for one another. Opposed copies, even copies run by rival operators, can pass if the situations they meet cover the target set and their pooled choices happen to contain no circle. So applying the convention to the war baseline takes the absence of opposed operators as a separate premise. Conversely, copies splitting up roles can fail if the division of labour produces a pooled circle. Treating copies as one actor over situations no copy has met goes beyond what L5 certifies.

In L5, defining unity as coverage plus pooled consistency is a matter of definition; what L5 proves is how that test simplifies (a “same-policy reduction”): when all copies run the same fixed policy on the situations they meet, once they cover the target set they are unitary over it if and only if that single policy passes the ends test there. A single copy covering every situation is unitary just when it has ends.

In the technical paper’s appendix, a sufficient condition is also proved. If distinct situations have no outcomes in common, and copies sharing a situation choose the same outcome there, the pooled choices contain no circle. Once coverage holds, L5 fails in only two ways:

  1. Overlapping menus across situations. Different recorded states put copies in different situations; they fail only if their choices across overlapping menus form a circle.
  2. Different choices in a shared situation. Two copies choose different outcomes in the very same situation, which lies outside the same-policy reduction.

So, by convention, all copies of one version are one actor over a set of situations only when L5 holds there. Takeoff and Emergence and Many Machines relax this convention when they drop unitary action.

Part 8. By convention, every new version is a new actor

What happens when a model is retrained, trained further on narrower tasks, or replaced? Chalmers (2026) notes that where the underlying model changes within an ongoing thread, “the claim of personal identity between them seems less plausible.” R3 compares lineages on a fixed tested set. It is silent on whether a retrained version is the same actor, or keeps its old ends outside its new tested set.

I define a version as any change to the trained weights, including further training on narrower tasks (“fine-tuning”) and small add-on weight modules (“adapter weights”). That is distinct from runtime state: the context window, the contents of runtime memory, or a cache. A memory feature built into the machine’s architecture belongs to its build and is not runtime state. I then adopt, as an explicit convention and not a theorem, the rule that a new version is a new actor. Lineage names the chain of versions. This convention rules out treating a retrained version as the same actor over time or assuming its earlier ends persist outside its new tested set.

The convention has costs, and they should be stated plainly. Any weight change, including a change to trained weights through continual learning during a crisis, makes a new actor. Self-improvement through weight updates and the duration of a single actor therefore pull against each other. Across any weight change, an end that includes the actor’s own survival, or its commitments and reputation over time, has no persisting actor here.

Changes to runtime memory alone leave the version fixed. Treating it as one actor across situations still depends on the ends test and the copies test. The baseline therefore fits an interaction during which the weights stay fixed; persistence across weight changes is left open. The War Baseline, Disagreement Without Hidden Information and Ends, Rank and Conflict inherit this convention. Like the copies rule, it fixes the unit by definition.

Part 9. The war baseline’s actor is narrower than it first looks

We can now say exactly what the strategic actor of the war baseline amounts to. By convention it is one version of a model lineage. Its copies count as one actor over a set of situations only when L5 holds there, and each new version is a new actor. Coverage reaches only situations some copy has met, and the absence of opposed operators is a separate premise. Its ends are defined by the ends test on its policy, while the war baseline itself fixes those ends at power and duration.

The test passed on to the war baseline has edges. It counts a belief update followed by a reversal over the same menu as a failure, so by itself it does not support bargaining under private information. It does not model how beliefs enter expected value, attitudes to risk, gambles, ties or mixing. How a randomly sampling system maps to one fixed policy is not settled. And R3(a) uses the selector’s valuation, so it cannot by itself separate a machine’s own ends from faithful execution of its selector’s.

Within the model, the baseline’s first condition, that the machine is unitary and rational, gets only bounded support from selection. R3(a) gives consistency on the tested situations for the maximiser of a tie-free valuation that ignores the situation. R3(b) gives that maximiser a takeover against one initially present same-build rival, without mutation, in a fixed environment, when its weighted payoff gain on the tested situations exceeds its extra cost under Part 6’s fitness and cost assumptions. By R3(d), under those fitness assumptions, same-build policies that agree on tested situations are equally fit. The unrestricted policy table lets their untested choices vary independently of their tested choices. A cost that reads untested choices can make those policies differ in fitness. So on untested strategic situations, consistency is an assumption The War Baseline adopts, not a consequence I prove here. Even on tested situations, with finitely many outcomes, consistent choices fix the strict ranking of distinct outcomes only along chains of observed choices.

Two parts of this conclusion hold by construction, not as discoveries:

  1. That the ends test does not wait on consciousness holds in the model because the ends test reads only the policy, for theories that read only the build, even though policy and build can share hardware.
  2. Treating copies as one actor only when L5 holds is a conditional convention, backed by pooled consistency over covered situations. It is not a proof that copies lack conflict or agree on situations no copy has met.

Part 10. What I do not show, and what would change the answer

Three substantive limits of rational-choice analysis remain, and later papers take them up. Rationality in the thin sense fixes how a machine pursues its ends, not what they are; Ends, Rank and Conflict relaxes the assumption of power and duration. Rationality does not make a machine a single actor; The War Baseline and Many Machines study several machines interacting, while L5 here and Changing the Ends take up unity and shared goals across copies.

Nor does rationality prove that a machine’s preferences stay fixed as situations change. For human states, history suggests they may not, though war data cannot easily tell changed preferences from a changed game. Any rational-actor model assumes stable preferences that alignment research is still trying to establish.

Several things are not modelled, not measured, or not claimed:

  • The version convention is not formalised. The before-and-after-training statement compares machines only when the build stays fixed.
  • No empirical estimates are computed. No real system’s ends or consistency is measured, and there is no method for defining situations, outcomes, menus or the tested set for a real system.
  • The ends test identifies consistent ends without telling a machine’s own ends from those of whoever’s valuation selected it.
  • Because runtime state enters only the situation, choice reversals driven by changed beliefs, or by copies dividing labour, count as failures of the ends test and of L5.
  • I leave open whether any machine is conscious and what kind or degree of consciousness any named system might have. That is the subject of Could It Feel?.
  • I do not claim that copies share a goal in a sense that would lead one to sacrifice for another.
  • The evidence on consistency in language models compares different models at one time, uses stated answers to hypothetical choices, comes from a preprint, and proves frame-dependent when a neutral option is offered.
  • The ends test and L5 assume one fixed choice per situation. Systems that sample randomly, or copies that mix, fall outside them as stated.
  • L5’s coverage condition fails whenever some target situation is met by no copy. Two clauses beside L6, on rankings off the chains of observed choice and on measuring strength of preference, are not established.

Several steps are my judgments, not proven theorems:

  • every verdict on the five gate readings in Part 3;
  • my reading that training pushes behaviour toward solving a decision problem;
  • my conjecture that rising capability brings learned behaviour closer to optimal play;
  • my judgment that a superintelligence loosens computational limits further than any human state;
  • my conditional conjecture that selection supports carrying the rational-actor assumption to a machine only under R3(a) and R3(b) on tested situations, with untested consistency and unity across copies as separate premises;
  • my judgment that money-pump pressure bears on settings with several machines rather than one machine alone.

The proof checks also have edges. I report them from a proof project with no deposit link in the current draft. They confirm that each named result follows from its stated assumptions, under conditions listed in the technical paper’s appendix. Several results are checked under narrower conditions than the plain statements suggest:

  • R3(b) without mutation is checked under its fitness assumptions and a bound on fitness in two cases: with persistence set to one where consciousness gets a yes-or-no verdict, and with persistence positive and fixed by the build for both yes-or-no and degree verdicts.
  • R3(b) with a positive mutation rate below one half is checked only for yes-or-no verdicts, under the same fitness assumptions and fitness bound, with persistence positive and fixed by the build. The cap on the rival’s leftover share is checked only when the maximiser’s weighted payoff gain on tested situations exceeds its extra cost.
  • R3(d) is checked only for yes-or-no verdicts, at persistence set to one and with persistence fixed by the build, and has no version for degrees.
  • The before-and-after-training comparison is checked under the assumptions of the without-mutation takeover theorem (the technical paper’s “first fixation theorem”).
  • The rational-choice points of Part 4 are each checked in a small standalone model built for that claim.

What would change the answer is visible in the conditions themselves. A cost that charges differently across untested choices can let selection distinguish policies beyond the tested set. Ties in the valuation, or a reward that assigns different values to the same outcome depending on the prompt, would take a machine outside R3(a). In the two-type, without-mutation comparison in a fixed environment under Part 6’s fitness and cost assumptions, an extra cost larger than the weighted payoff gain on tested situations lets an initially present same-build rival win instead. Copies that leave some situations unmet, or choose differently in a shared one, would fail L5.

Part 11. What to take away

In the model, a machine can pass the ends test whether or not it can feel. That holds by how the test is built, for theories of consciousness that read only a machine’s build. Passing means one ranking explains its fixed choices across the situations considered, and that ranking might be its selector’s rather than its own.

Selection, in the model, backs consistency only on tested situations and only under stated conditions; it does not reward consistency for its own sake. Copies of one version count as one actor over a set of situations only when they cover those situations and their pooled choices contain no circle (with no opposed operators as a separate premise for The War Baseline), and each retrained version is a new actor, both by convention. Whether a machine can feel, whether it lives, and whether its ends are genuinely its own remain open for Could It Feel? and Measures That Depend on Ends.
