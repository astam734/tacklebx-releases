In brief I build a mathematical model of selection among AI model lineages and ask when it would spread the kinds of organisation that seventeen theories of consciousness require. The baseline model compares two types on fixed hardware, with fixed pressure weights, some selection and some weight on the lineage’s own fitness. With no mutation, an idealisation outside the deployment dial, the type that does better after accounting for survival takes over from a mixed start. With equal two-way mutation at a positive rate below one half, the population settles into a single mix and that type is the majority. At equal survival, what matters is the gain in weighted benefits, minus the extra cost, plus any change in the direct effect of consciousness. When both types fall below a theory’s cutoff for how closely they must meet its conditions, consciousness makes no difference to their relative fitness. With no task payoff depending on it, a costly signal of good and bad—counted as valenced feeling only on five of the theories—is selected away at equal survival when its consciousness bonus is below its extra cost, yet spreads for any non-negative consciousness weight within the model’s bound when its survival ratio beats its zero-bonus fitness disadvantage. Ten of the seventeen theories (six of thirteen without four provisional ones) rule out conventional digital hardware outright, and that is a property of the list of theories, not a measurement. When consciousness has no direct effect on fitness, the model’s own commitments mean that watching machines evolve leaves our weights across theories unchanged, under its stated conditions. No named system is assessed, and whether any machine feels stays open.

Part 1. Could selection on machines do what it may have done for animals?

Natural selection may have shaped the nervous systems of mammals and birds. The proposed pressures were the need to learn across gaps in time, to regulate a vulnerable body, to pool scarce perceptual capacity, to predict a changing world and to navigate social life. I ask what would follow if a similar kind of selection acted on AI systems.

The units are lineages. A lineage is a deployed AI model and its successors, copied across many machines and replaced release by release. In the model, a population of such lineages runs on one fixed class of hardware. The question is what share of that population comes to be organised the way each theory of consciousness requires, in comparisons between two types. A second question is what measurements of machines can say about that share.

Selection is studied on its own here. The model leaves open how to treat a lineage that is first trained by imitating human text and then selected across generations in a market. Part 14 returns to this.

The question breaks into five:

  1. When does selection spread the organisation a theory requires? (Part 6)
  2. When does selection favour an internal signal of good and bad, and when does a lineage have something to lose? (Parts 5 and 7)
  3. What can measurements of machines say about which theory is right, or where its threshold sits? (Part 9)
  4. What does it cost to focus on felt experience rather than on how information is shared inside a system? (Part 2)
  5. What follows for tests that watch behaviour, inspect mechanisms or steer internal states? (Part 12)

The model keeps two things apart: theorems about how selection changes how common each organisation is, and each theory’s verdict on a given organisation and hardware. In the technical paper, the main text states results informally, and its appendices give the exact definitions and proofs. Many results were checked by a proof-checking program called Lean. Such a program confirms that each conclusion follows from the stated assumptions, not that the assumptions hold in the world. The model places no named AI system anywhere on its scales.

Three building blocks come from When Is a Machine an Actor?, my paper on when an AI system counts as a single strategic actor:

  • All copies of a deployed lineage are treated as one actor. The check is whether the copies’ pooled choices are consistent across the situations they cover.
  • Selection fixes what a system is after only in the situations where it was tested. It makes the system’s choices consistent only at the optimum.
  • The strategic actor in The War Baseline, my paper on strategic conflict, does not need to be conscious. This holds by construction, not as an empirical finding. What a system is after is defined on its behaviour. Every theory of consciousness in the roster is defined on its internal structure and its hardware.

To keep things concrete, Parts 5 to 7 follow one running example. Two organisations share all their background architecture and differ only in whether they carry a signal of good and bad. The setting is idealised: how much each selective pressure counts stays fixed, and mutation goes on at a rate below one half. In the illustrative profile of real deployment described in Part 5, those weights instead shift with the state of the population.

Part 2. This guide follows felt experience, which no machine measurement sees directly

Two ideas of consciousness are often run together. Phenomenal consciousness asks whether there is something it is like to be the system: whether it has felt experience. Sentience is the valenced kind of feeling, which is good or bad for the one who has it. Access consciousness is different. It means information made widely available inside a system for reasoning, control and report. A fourth idea is a system’s model of its own mental states, which I call its self-model.

The roster of seventeen theories covers both main ideas. Global workspace theory and the two attention schema theories answer for access, and the attention schema theories also answer for the self-model. Some proponents read global workspace theory as a theory of felt experience too. Higher-order thought theory, recurrent processing theory, illusionism and every theory that also puts conditions on the material the system runs on—the hybrid and substrate families introduced in Part 3—answer for felt experience. Solms’s predictive-processing account and the body-centred hybrid theories answer for sentience. Part 4 describes each theory.

I focus on felt experience and sentience. The welfare questions of Part 11 turn on whether a system feels. So do the moral-status questions examined in Measures That Depend on Ends, my paper on governance measures that depend on reading what a system wants. Take any finite list of candidate organisations whose relevant profiles can be told apart by measurements reflecting a system’s own organisation rather than imitated text. On that list, the measurements fix how far each system meets each access theory’s conditions (its “degree”, explained in Part 4) before any threshold applies. Given that condition, access alone adds little to the problem of what is hidden.

The cost of this focus is that felt experience is never directly observed in a machine. It enters the model only through what humans report about themselves (Part 3). So every claim about felt experience depends on which theory is true and on how much weight we start by giving each theory. In the technical paper these weights are called the prior; here I call them the starting weights. Measurements of machines read only how a system is organised and how it behaves.

In the model, this has three consequences:

  • On a finite list of candidate organisations whose relevant profiles are told apart by measurements reflecting a system’s own organisation rather than imitated text, those measurements fix how far each system meets each theory’s conditions before thresholds apply.
  • For a single system observed at one moment, measurements never read a theory’s threshold. They also leave the starting weights unchanged, but only under an independence assumption that I take as a hypothesis and do not prove. So when theories that carry some weight disagree about a system, the weighted average verdict stays strictly between the lowest and highest of their verdicts. In that average, each theory is evaluated for what it targets—access, the self-model, felt experience or sentience.
  • Watching selection over time does no better when consciousness itself adds nothing to fitness. Under the model’s commitments and the conditions set out in Part 9 (a finite, theory-free starting spread over the model’s settings and starting state, for observations of nonzero chance), observations then leave the starting weights unchanged. They can move them only when consciousness has a direct effect on fitness. A theory’s threshold stays where its starting weights put it, except under conditions argued in Part 9.

So under those conditions, when consciousness adds nothing directly to fitness and theories that disagree about a system each carry some weight, the overall verdict on that system (each theory evaluated for what it targets) remains an average over theories. It is not an empirical finding.

Part 3. Which theory is right is treated as unknown, checked against human report

Human report is the only independent check on consciousness (Bayne et al. 2024; Birch, Ginsburg & Jablonka 2020; Key 2016). As Bayne et al. (2024) put it, “we have no independent way of assessing whether the members of the target population are conscious – and if we did, we would not need C-tests.” Birch, Ginsburg and Jablonka (2020) validate their markers in humans “because it is only in humans that we can independently verify, through verbal report.”

In the model, human report anchors the evidence. It does not define consciousness. I assume that, for every theory except illusionism, its condition holds in people who can report their experience. Illusionism needs no such bridge, since on that view there is no felt experience beyond its functional seeming (Frankish 2016). If saying you are aware defined consciousness, the analysis would be committed in advance to higher-order theories. It also could not use animal evidence as a check.

A system trained by imitating human text is a special case. For such a system, a fluent report of inner life is the cheapest output to produce. That is why the model puts imitation-trained behaviour on separate branches (Part 9).

How far the anchor reaches beyond humans is contested. Bayne et al. (2024) start from agreed human cases and move to harder ones, such as infants, fetuses and disorders of consciousness. From there they work outward to animals and artificial systems. They note the worry that this is “unacceptably anthropocentric.” Part of their reply is that complexity measures agreeing across sleep, anaesthesia and coma offer “some optimism.” They also say one could argue that there is as yet no evidence that consciousness can be realised in different physical ways. Andrews and Delmore, reporting Andrews (2024), instead start from “the supposition that animals are conscious.” They study the dimensions of consciousness within that framework.

Others support strong credence—degree of belief, or probability weight—in mammal and bird consciousness, including the New York Declaration (2024) and the Cambridge Declaration (2012). Chandaria et al. (2026) wrote a book whose authors are affiliated with an AI developer and are a party to the debate. They ask that indicators be validated on “mammals, birds, cephalopods,” because “[i]f the indicators cannot discriminate where we have reasonable confidence, they cannot be trusted where we do not” (Chandaria et al. 2026). Their one worked example of updating a belief about which level matters concerns a human case.

I take human report as the formal anchor, and hold mammal and bird consciousness as a strong check on the starting weights, not a hard rule, because a hard rule would rule out higher-order theories by fiat. Within humans, theories can be told apart only where the brain’s functional organisation varies across experimental conditions.

No theory of consciousness commands consensus. The only adversarial test so far challenged key tenets of both leading theories (Cogitate Consortium 2025). Adopting a single role theory would leave the model open to that theory’s critics. Nor do I restrict the space to Block’s two-way split between functional role and biological substrate. That split is one party’s framing, and it is a special case of a richer space.

Instead, the model treats the true theory as unknown and spreads its starting weights across three families:

  • Role theories: the right functional organisation is enough, on any hardware.
  • Hybrid theories: they need both an organisation, usually tied to keeping a body going, and a non-digital or living substrate.
  • Substrate theories: physical properties of the hardware do the work. Each is paired with a minimal role condition, so the right hardware is necessary but not enough. In the technical paper these are called realizer theories.

Theories in a family share its weight equally. The starting weights also range over each theory’s threshold (Part 4). Every theorem is proved for one fixed theory, or for all theories at once, before any averaging over theories.

Part 4. In the roster, ten of seventeen theories rule out conventional computers

What the hardware is made of

The model describes a system by two things: its hardware and its organisation. Hardware comes in five classes: chemical tissue, electrical tissue, digital hardware, neuromorphic chips and analog hardware. Whole-brain emulation counts as a way of designing a system, not as a separate class.

Each class has a copy fidelity, meaning how exactly what runs on it can be copied. Fidelity is full on digital hardware, where weights can run on “a different physical copy of the hardware” (Hinton 2022). It is zero on living tissue. It is below full on analog hardware, whose settings drift (Mehonic 2020). Each class also has nine yes-or-no physical properties. It can be living, self-producing, physically integrated through hardware feedback, able to signal chemically at connections between cells, coupled through electromagnetic fields, or carbon-based. The model also records whether quantum states in the hardware can coexist and collapse to one outcome. It can have the brain’s specific causal powers in Searle’s sense. And it can sense through membrane chemistry.

Isn’t the brain analog and a computer digital? Less cleanly than it seems. “Almost everybody accepts that the brain does a tremendous amount of analog processing” (Faisal 2008; Sarpeshkar 1998), yet spikes fire all-or-none. The brain mixes graded computation inside neurons with “more digital” signalling between them (Faisal 2008; Sarpeshkar 1998). Infinite shades of grey are not available either: noise “cannot be removed from a signal once it has been added” (Faisal 2008; Sarpeshkar 1998). The time between a brain cell’s spikes can carry information. For cells in the cortex, model estimates put the first interval at fewer than 7.5 bits of information and later intervals at considerably less (Levy & Calvert 2021). Analog silicon exists. On the digital side, choosing each next output step by step (“decoding”) uses randomness in open-ended generation, while always picking the single top-scoring output (“greedy decoding”) is deterministic (Holtzman 2020), the 16-bit number format makes sufficiently tiny values zero (Micikevicius 2018), and without a fixed order of addition, identical runs are “completely non-reproducible” (Shanmugavelu 2024).

What survives of the contrast is a difference in where variation comes from. Neural noise comes from molecules jostling at body temperature. Digital circuits restore signals to zero or one at each stage, and they “are engineered not to change continually in slight ways from their mere operation” (Godfrey-Smith 2016).

Whether that difference matters for experience is the core dispute. Chalmers (1995) argues from gradually replacing neurons with silicon that function is what counts. Godfrey-Smith (2016) replies that as living parts are replaced, the system “must behave more and more differently as well.” Seth (2025), a party to the debate, observes that all known conscious systems are “biological and alive.” Klatzmann and Doerig (2026) reply that all known ones also live on Earth.

How the system is organised

On the other side, organisation is a set of structural features. Each has a degree from zero to one. The model also records whether that degree is above zero. The features are:

  • global broadcast, meaning information pooled and shared across modules;
  • recurrence within a single step (none, cycling in the algorithm, or cycling physically in the hardware), within an episode and across episodes;
  • models of the system itself, of its own attention and of others;
  • a valence signal, an internal signal of good and bad linked to survival;
  • a stake, meaning regulation of a variable whose failure ends the lineage;
  • sustained rather than episodic activity.

There is also a marker for unlimited associative learning, used for non-designed living systems and not as a theory. The organisation also includes a policy: the fixed rule mapping situations to actions.

Neuroscience draws the line around recurrence sharply. In the cortex, the brain’s outer layer, signals first pass forward in 100 to 200 milliseconds. On Lamme’s account, “[f]eedforward processing itself is unconscious” (Lamme 2018). Feedback follows, and general anaesthesia abolishes feedback between brain areas while sparing the first sweep (Lamme 2018). Aru (2023) notes that “[t]he structure of the thalamocortical system does not change when we are in deep sleep or undergo anesthesia, yet consciousness disappears.” A deployed neural network’s activity, by contrast, “consists of temporally discrete forward passes” (Butlin et al. 2023). Whether a software loop across steps counts as recurrence is contested, so the roster carries it as an open switch.

The “basic role” for Searle’s and Block’s theories defaults to the global workspace condition. That way the right hardware is necessary but not enough. A few notes on particular rows follow:

  • Higher-order thought. This row reads the general self-model feature. So the prediction and social pressures raise its degree, and its verdict once that degree passes the cutoff, by routes that higher-order theories themselves do not name.
  • Illusionism. Chandaria et al. (2026) note a dissenting reading on which the question survives as what mechanisms produce the impression of experience. That reading would reduce to the attention-schema condition. It is recorded as a dissent, not a separate row.
  • Seth’s biological naturalism. A weaker variant needing only valence and stake, with no hardware veto, is noted but not added. Seth holds that it is “hard and perhaps impossible” to separate what brains do from what they are.
  • Enactivism. Whether self-production rules out computation is contested between Rosen’s claim that life is uncomputable and Varela’s view that self-production can run in a cellular automaton—a grid updated by simple rules—as Thompson (2004) reports.
  • Integrated information. On standard graphics chips, the transistors of one parallel attention unit (“attention head”) are not reciprocally constrained by another’s (Chandaria et al. 2026).
  • MEAT. Block illustrates his conjecture with comb jellies, whose largely electrical nerve nets did not lead to animals generally considered candidates for consciousness. A single contrast between animal groups cannot separate electrochemistry from other differences, so the comb jelly serves only as an illustration. Box jellyfish have electrochemical nerves, yet some researchers also deny that they are conscious (Merker 2007; Barron & Klein 2016). Block grants that “we do not know why” chemical transmission won (Block 2026).
  • EM. McFadden (2020) was read in full. His exclusion covers a computer that “computes solely through matter,” and he offers “a route towards artificial consciousness through designing an EM field-sensitive computer.” The row’s zero applies to conventional hardware.

Four hardware-constraint rows—CARBON and IONIC in the hybrid family, EM and QM in the substrate family—are provisional by ruling. CARBON, IONIC and QM still rely on secondary reporting in Chandaria et al. (2026). So every count is reported both with and without these four.

Degree, threshold and gate

Whether consciousness comes by degrees or switches on is disputed. Godfrey-Smith (2020, 2023a) argues that if experience is wholly physical, it arose by degrees with “no ‘lights on’ moment.” Barron and Klein (2016) argue for “a definite cutoff point.” The model allows both without assuming either.

Each theory gives a system a degree between zero and one. It is how far the organisation meets the theory’s conditions, multiplied by a yes-or-no for whether the hardware passes. Hardware stays yes-or-no because Chandaria et al. (2026) treat substrate theories as “cross-cutting realisability constraints” that “can veto an attribution.” Birch, Schnell and Clayton (2020) propose five dimensions of consciousness: perceptual richness, evaluative richness, unity, temporality and selfhood. No source gives a rule for combining them into one number, so I treat them as what each theory’s single degree grades.

Each theory also has a threshold. A gate turns the degree into the final verdict. No source says what shape a consciousness gate has, so the model carries two forms side by side and reports wherever they differ:

  • Step gate: zero below the threshold, then a jump to the full degree as soon as the threshold is reached.
  • Ramp gate: for any threshold below one, zero up to and at the threshold, then a steady climb that reaches one at full degree (and defined equal to the step gate when the threshold is one).

As Figure 1 shows, both gates give zero below the threshold, and both reduce to the plain degree when the threshold is zero. They differ when the degree equals a threshold between zero and one, where the step gate is open and the ramp gate is shut. If every degree is just zero or one, both gates give the same verdict at any threshold, so the simple yes-or-no model fits inside the graded one.

The starting weights spread over thresholds as well as theories:

  • The naturalistic dual-aspect theory explicitly denies a threshold, so its threshold is zero.
  • Higher-order thought is all-or-none, since a higher-order representation is present or absent. It gets one shared positive cutoff. That cutoff is below one.
  • Illusionism gets no split, since its degree is always zero.
  • Every other theory splits its weight equally between no threshold and the shared cutoff.

Neither the threshold values nor the gate forms have an empirical source. None of the sources rostered for integrated information theory supplies a positive cutoff. Putting it in the split class, rather than treating any positive integration as counting, is my choice.

What the count shows, and what it is not

Ten of the seventeen rows rule out conventional digital hardware through their hardware test: all five hybrid and all five substrate rows. Without the four provisional rows, it is six of thirteen. Illusionism is zero on every kind of hardware. That makes eleven of seventeen rows that give conventional digital hardware a zero verdict, or seven of thirteen without the provisional rows. This holds for every threshold and on both gates.

An optional switch, off by default, reads global workspace and recurrent processing theories as requiring physically integrated hardware (Chandaria et al. 2026). With the switch on, they also exclude digital hardware, and the total with illusionism rises to thirteen of seventeen, or nine of thirteen.

When a theory’s hardware test fails, its degree and verdict are zero for every organisation. The conscious share of the population then stays zero under every selection regime, from every starting population, at every time. The exception is selection acting across hardware types, so that the hardware itself evolves.

This count is a fixed property of the roster and of how its families are weighted. It is not a measured frequency of non-conscious machines. Across the five hardware classes, several rows behave identically in the version that scores each feature yes or no:

  • Seth’s biological naturalism and enactivism coincide, because living matches self-producing. CARBON and IONIC coincide too. Each pair takes two-fifths of the hybrid family’s weight. In living tissue with the stake feature present, both pairs agree with each other as well.
  • Integrated information, EM and QM can coincide, depending on the hardware’s integration, field and quantum properties. Together they take up to three-fifths of the substrate family’s weight.

Equal weights within a family make a credence depend on how theories happen to be divided into rows. Adding the four provisional rows leaves credence on digital hardware unchanged, since only the role family admits digital hardware. With family weights fixed, though, it dilutes each existing hybrid and substrate row from one-third to one-fifth. That can lower total credence on neuromorphic or analog hardware without field coupling, while the EM row can raise it on field-coupled hardware.

Part 5. On an illustrative scoring, AI lineages are selected, but developers and markets hold much of the say

How Darwinian is the process?

Following Godfrey-Smith (2011, reporting his 2009 framework), I grade a selection regime on five dials from zero to three:

  • variation;
  • heritability;
  • differential reproduction;
  • whether small changes to a lineage tend to make small differences to its fitness;
  • how much fitness depends on a lineage’s own character rather than luck.

I add two dials for artificial systems. One is whether peer competition or an outside designer sets the payoffs. The other is learning within a lifetime.

Machine paradigms score very differently. In Ray’s (1991) Tierra, self-copying programs mutate, compete for processor time and memory, and face a “reaper” queue. Tierra sits nearest the biological case, though its organisms are tiny and do not learn within a lifetime. Birch’s (2024) simulated 410-neuron robot controllers, whose weights stay fixed over a lifetime, share that limit.

Evolution strategies nudge one set of parameters under a designer’s measure (Salimans et al. 2017). Population-based training copies and perturbs the better training runs (Jaderberg et al. 2017). In multi-agent hide-and-seek, self-play produced “six emergent phases in agent strategy …, each of which creates a new pressure for the opposing team to adapt” (Baker et al. 2020).

In the model, selection acts on deployed lineages. Released models are copied through deployment and compete across successive generations. Hendrycks (2023) argues that competitive AI products meet the classic conditions for evolution because the Price equation, which describes change under selection, “only requires similarity between iterations.”

My scoring of deployment is an illustrative judgment, with no scoring rubric:

  • variation 3;
  • heritability 2, which means mutation is always ongoing;
  • differential reproduction 3;
  • dependence on the lineage’s own character 2;
  • payoffs set by peers 2;
  • lifetime learning 3;
  • the fitness landscape unscored.

The score of 2 on own character is low, below full autonomy at 3. A lineage’s reproduction depends on developer and market decisions, not on its own character alone. So the own-character dial, which I call the developer dial, gives intrinsic fitness strictly less than full weight.

The links between learning and evolution are partly formal and partly analogy. The standard equation for how fitter types grow in share (the replicator equation) links mathematically to updating beliefs from evidence (Bayesian updating; Harper 2009). Weak selection with genetic reshuffling across parents (recombination) matches a known weight-updating learning rule (Chastain et al. 2014). Training one neural network by nudging its weights downhill on an error score (stochastic gradient descent), though, is only an analogy to evolution. One architecture’s weights never reproduce as competing individuals, and they never undergo a major evolutionary transition in Szathmáry’s (2015) sense. Training on a fixed dataset is also not open-ended to an observer who can model that dataset (Hughes et al. 2024).

Three routes, and post-training is not selection

The model separates three ways a system’s organisation can come about:

  • Selection on its own: the shares of organisations in the population change from generation to generation.
  • Imitation: a network is trained on human text. This sets how its behaviour can be read (Part 9). It does not move population shares by selection.
  • Design: a deliberate jump to a target organisation. Whole-brain emulation is an example, and it assumes the organisation relevant to consciousness can be realised on different hardware (Sandberg & Bostrom 2008).

Reinforcement post-training of an existing lineage splits two ways. On the policy branch, it changes only the policy. The structure stays the same, so every theory’s degree and verdict stay the same. On the structure branch, a developer changes at least one structural feature, which is a design move. Neither branch is a selection step, and neither is a developer’s structural change between releases. By assumption, neither changes which of the three route labels (such as imitation) the system carries when its observations are evaluated.

What fitness is made of

The model starts intrinsic fitness at one. To calculate the adjustment, it adds the weighted benefits from learning, valence, attention, prediction and social life, subtracts the cost of the organisation and its training, and includes any direct contribution from consciousness. Selection strength scales this net contribution, measured relative to a fixed payoff scale, before it is added to the starting value.

I call the weight the model puts on the theory’s verdict the consciousness weight. It marks a classic divide in the philosophy of mind. When it is zero, we are on the epiphenomenalist’s branch (Robinson 2023). Consciousness itself adds nothing beyond the functional benefits of the architecture beneath it. When it is positive, we are on James’s branch (James 1879). On this branch, felt experience directly raises reproductive success. James saw consciousness as “an organ added for the sake of steering a nervous system grown too complex to regulate itself”.

The developer dial then mixes this intrinsic fitness with a fixed baseline set by developers and markets, giving effective fitness. Finally, effective fitness is multiplied by the lineage’s chance of surviving each generation, which I call its persistence.

Having something to lose

An analogy from biology frames this. An animal’s body is precarious: it must keep essential variables within tight bounds or die. In the model, the matching constraint for a lineage is persistence. Persistence is the chance per generation that the lineage survives, given how it regulates an essential variable against running out of energy or being shut down.

Persistence multiplies fitness outside the developer dial. There are two reasons. A lineage that is shut down or runs out of budget gets no copies from developers either. And placing persistence inside the dial would break a key result about uniform hazards, described below, whenever the dial is below full, which is the deployment case.

Persistence depends only on structure. It reads the valence signal, the stake feature and the energy cost relative to the energy budget. It never reads the policy, the attention model or the model of others. Having nothing to lose means persistence is full for every organisation.

Two results show when shutdown risk and energy limits matter for selection:

  • A uniform risk is not a stake. If every organisation faces the same shutdown hazard, the population follows exactly the path it would follow with no hazard at all. A lineage has something to lose if and only if its persistence varies with its organisation.
  • Persistence selects even when developers decide everything else. Even if developers set reproduction so that intrinsic fitness plays no role, the population still moves by mutation and persistence, not by mutation alone.

Whether a machine has something to lose splits into three cases:

  1. One running copy on copyable hardware. With perfect, cheap copying, ending one running copy does not end the lineage, because the weights stay “immortal” across hardware copies (Hinton 2022). A single copy’s energy or shutdown is then not an essential variable for the lineage, unless rules or costs restrict copying. That is a fact about the governance regime, not about the silicon.
  2. The whole lineage, on any hardware. A deployed lineage ends when its energy and compute costs exceed what developers and markets will pay. A digital lineage can have this essential variable. It counts as a stake in the model’s sense only if the lineage’s own valence signal or stake regulation moves its persistence. If an outside developer choice sets the same risk for every organisation, that risk cancels.
  3. Mortal hardware and simulated reapers. Analog and memristor hardware whose settings drift carries a built-in constraint on each copy (Mehonic 2020). Neuromorphic chips qualify as mortal computers in Ororbia and Friston’s (2023) sense only if self-regulating imperatives are built into them. Simulated self-regulating agents have a stake only if leaving their bounds removes the lineage. A Tierra-like rule that deletes a lineage when its energy stock runs out would do this (Ray 1991). Costs drain the stock and payoffs replenish it.

That stock-and-reaper rule depends on history. It is kept for discussion and test design, not formalised in the model. The model does not decide, for Tierra or any named system, whether persistence runs through valence.

Part 6. At equal persistence and fixed weights, a feature spreads only when its gains plus any bonus beat its cost, and below a threshold consciousness adds nothing

Five proposed pressures

Five selective pressures have been proposed to explain why consciousness-related architectures evolved:

  • Flexible learning. Ginsburg and Jablonka’s unlimited associative learning links new, compound stimuli across gaps in time. Birch, Ginsburg and Jablonka (2020) argue that it requires a consensus cluster of consciousness markers. They conjecture that it arose from the Cambrian in most vertebrates, some arthropods and the octopus–squid group.
  • Valence and bodily regulation. In laboratory trade-offs, forty-one bumblebees from eight colonies avoided 55 °C heated feeders less often when the feeders held richer sugar water. This is behaviour “consistent with” felt pain, though “not a formal proof” (Gibbons et al. 2022). Godfrey-Smith (2016) treats pain and primordial emotions as old forms of experience “not apparently reliant on centralization.” Solms (2019) argues that felt valence lets an organism regulate departures from its set points “in unpredicted contexts,” and concedes that predictable settings can be handled automatically. Panksepp (2011) points to conserved reward-and-punishment circuits deep in the mammalian brain. They survive early removal of the cortex, and people report feelings when the circuits are stimulated. LeDoux and Brown (2017) contest this and treat those circuits as supplying only “nonconscious inputs.”
  • Attention and global broadcast. Webb and Graziano (2015) argue that awareness is a simplified model of attention, built because a model improves control. People unaware of a cue leave attention “stuck on the location of the subliminal stimulus.” Dehaene, Lau and Kouider (2017) argue that pooling many specialised modules into one decision is a problem “faced by any animal or autonomous AI system,” solved by a limited-capacity global workspace. Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini (2024) dispute the capacity limits, and Godfrey-Smith (2016) questions the level of detail at which metabolism matters.
  • Prediction and self-modelling. Seth and Tsakiris (2018) ground selfhood in prediction that serves the body’s regulation. Thompson (2004) ties valence to self-maintenance without prediction.
  • Social life. Humphrey (1976) argues that primate intellect was selected to manage social life. His current account makes phenomenal experience “our ticket to having a theory of mind and living in what I have called ‘the society of selves’” (Humphrey 2025), restricted to mammals and birds by warm blood. Graziano and Kastner (2011) propose that machinery for tracking others’ attention is reused to model one’s own. Oakley and Halligan (2017) argue that selection favoured communicating an inner narrative, with experience a passive accompaniment.

In the model, learning favours unlimited associative learning, bodily regulation favours valence, and attention favours broadcast and a model of one’s own attention. Prediction favours a self-model, while social pressure favours models of oneself and others.

The basic result

The basic result (Result R1 in the technical paper) says when selection on one hardware class spreads any such feature. Compare two organisations that differ in one structural feature, such as having a global workspace. The result holds under four conditions:

  • how much each pressure counts stays fixed;
  • selection has some strength;
  • the developer dial gives intrinsic fitness some weight;
  • the two organisations have equal persistence. This is automatic for any feature that the valence signal, the stake feature and energy cost do not involve.

The feature-carrier’s advantage then has exactly the sign of one net margin. The margin is the pressure-weighted functional gain, minus the extra cost, plus the consciousness weight times the change in the theory’s verdict.

What happens next depends on mutation:

  • With no mutation, the feature-carrier takes over from every mixed start if the margin is positive, dies out if it is negative, and holds steady if it is zero. No mutation lies outside the deployment dial’s range, since deployment has ongoing mutation.
  • With equal two-way mutation at a positive rate below one half, the population settles from every start at a single mix of the two types. The feature-carrier is a majority in that mix if and only if its total fitness is higher, and when its total fitness is higher that mix approaches all feature-carriers as mutation vanishes.

Under those conditions and with no consciousness bonus, a workspace spreads if and only if its pressure-weighted gain beats its extra cost. When the background also meets the workspace theory’s recurrence requirement, the same holds for access consciousness under that theory. On James’s branch, the direct bonus lowers that cost barrier by the consciousness weight times the change in verdict. This is a margin on cost, not the threshold of Part 4.

The result also names its own failure cases:

  • A pressure the model leaves out can penalise the feature, for instance if prediction gets worse with broadcast. The margin can then be negative even when attention alone beats the cost.
  • With no mutation, there is no stable mix unless fitnesses are equal.
  • With zero selection strength, intrinsic fitness is constant. Differences in survival can still matter.
  • When developers set reproduction entirely, the population moves only by mutation and persistence.

The valley at the threshold

Grading consciousness by degree leaves these two-type results intact when the threshold is zero; it only rescales them. This applies to the comparisons about spreading a feature, spreading valence and social modelling. A positive threshold changes things. It creates what I call the valley at the threshold. This is a plateau that is flat in the consciousness bonus only, while total fitness can still vary along it.

Take two organisations that both sit below a theory’s threshold. Both verdicts are zero on either gate, so the consciousness bonus contributes nothing to their difference, however large the consciousness weight is. Now suppose each mutation can change the degree by only a limited amount. Divide the threshold by the largest allowed change and round up to a whole number. A lineage starting at zero needs at least that many steps. Until it first reaches the threshold, every step lands below it.

As Figure 2 shows, the climb from zero to the gate takes at least five mutations in that illustration. Between two organisations below the gate, consciousness contributes nothing to their fitness difference (so at equal persistence, under the basic result’s conditions, only functional gains and costs decide). With positive mutation, a chain of mutations the model allows can carry a type across the gate. Crossing is not proved blocked.

Once across, for any positive threshold below one, the two gates diverge below full degree. On the step gate, the lineage gains the full bonus for its degree at once. On the ramp gate, its bonus climbs from zero. The jump represents Barron and Klein’s (2016) “definite cutoff”, and the climb represents Godfrey-Smith’s (2023a) gradual account. I add the limit on how much a single mutation can change.

Part 7. With fixed weights and no task payoff for valence, costly valence spreads only if feeling pays enough directly or the lineage has something to lose

Return to the running example: two organisations differing only in the valence signal, with fixed pressure weights and mutation below one half. Two further assumptions apply. No task payoff depends on valence, which suits predictable settings, where Solms (2019) concedes that regulation can be automatic. And valence costs something extra. Solms’s thesis that felt valence pays in unpredicted settings enters the model through persistence, not through a task payoff.

Under these conditions, the valence-carrier takes over from a mixed start with no mutation, and settles into a majority under equal two-way mutation at a positive rate below one half, exactly when its persistence times its effective fitness beats its rival’s. The answer then splits on whether persistence depends on valence.

At equal survival, valence is selected away if feeling pays less than it costs

Suppose the two organisations have equal persistence. Then cost and the consciousness bonus decide. Valence is selected away whenever the consciousness weight times the gain in verdict is smaller than valence’s extra cost. With equal two-way mutation, its eventual share is bounded above by a constant times the mutation rate. That constant depends on the two types’ fitnesses. It is driven to zero as mutation vanishes.

That cost condition is needed. Take Solms’s theory in the running example, where hardware passes. In the yes-or-no case where the valence-carrier gets a full verdict of one against zero, if the consciousness weight is a strong 0.5 and valence costs only 0.1 extra, the net margin is 0.4, which is positive. Valence then settles as a majority with ongoing mutation, or takes over with no mutation, even with nothing to lose.

Below a positive threshold, there is no consciousness bonus to weigh against the extra cost. If both organisations sit below the gate, the bonus vanishes on both gate forms. Valence is then selected away whatever the consciousness weight. Below the threshold, James’s branch and the epiphenomenalist’s branch give the same answer.

Extending the small-share bound to all backgrounds at once, across the five valence theories, would need a uniform cost-margin hypothesis. That extension remains a conjecture.

Something to lose: valence can spread with no bonus at all

Now suppose the valence signal regulates an essential variable, so that the valence-carrier survives shutdown or energy exhaustion more often. Specifically, suppose the ratio of the valence-carrier’s survival rate to its rival’s is larger than the ratio of the rival’s effective fitness to the valence-carrier’s when the consciousness bonus is zero.

Every theory’s verdict either rises or stays flat as the valence degree rises. A proof-checking program confirmed this. Given the survival condition, the valence-carrier is then a majority with ongoing mutation, and takes over with no mutation, for every non-negative consciousness weight within the model’s fitness bound. That includes a weight of zero. This full conclusion was machine-checked when degrees are just zero or one. In the graded case, it is argued from the checked facts.

A worked case: under Solms’s theory, let the valence-carrier always survive and its rival survive half the time. Set the cost to one tenth for the valence-carrier and zero for its rival. Survival then outweighs cost, and valence prevails both with no bonus and with a weight of one half.

Isn’t persistence just a lower cost under another name? A critic might say that a survival factor that multiplies fitness is equivalent to a smaller cost. In the model it is not. Persistence multiplies effective fitness outside the developer dial. That keeps the uniform-hazard result when the dial is below full, and it still selects when developers decide everything else. In practice, measuring compute is not the same as recovering a model’s full cost against its task benefits. Shutdown and budget-exhaustion events track persistence directly, though shutdown counts alone cannot show whether valence or stake, rather than developer decisions, changed it.

So the model states when valence spreads. For the five theories that address sentience, valence can contribute to a sentience verdict if the theory’s other requirements and the gate allow it. It does not claim that deployed lineages meet those conditions.

Part 8. In the model, social pressure can hold a population mixed or split it by history

The earlier results assumed fixed pressure weights. Social life is different: what a model of others is worth can depend on how many other lineages have one. The social result (Result R2 in the technical paper) compares organisations with and without both a model of others and a model of their own attention, the features the two attention schema theories require. Persistence reads neither feature, so it cancels.

This result assumes no mutation, which lies outside the deployment dial’s range. The analysis covers that case only. It assumes a common background, positive selection strength and some weight on a lineage’s own fitness. It also assumes the payoff difference changes in a straight line as modellers become more common. Three regimes follow.

When modelling pays while rare and loses when common. There is then a single balanced mix. In continuous time, the population moves to that mix exponentially fast from every mixed start for every positive selection strength below one. This is proved for a positive time-rescaling of the dynamics—with the time-rescaling itself not formalised—and assumes a solution exists, which is standard and was taken as given in the proof checker. In discrete generations with no mutation, the mix is locally stable—meaning the population returns to it from nearby starts—when payoffs vary without jumps at the mix and the rule taking one generation’s share to the next (the “map”) has a slope there of size below one, for which selection strength below two thirds is enough. Under the same continuity at the mix, the mix is unstable when that slope has size above one, while a slope of size one is left undecided. If payoffs vary without jumps across the whole range and a higher share in one generation always leads to a higher share in the next, the population reaches the mix from every mixed start.

The two-thirds guarantee cannot be raised. In a constructed example with a kink in the payoffs and a selection strength of 0.8, the slope at the balanced mix is minus three, so the mix is unstable. Smooth payoffs within the model’s bounds cannot reach that slope in this example. Even so, at any selection strength above two thirds, some smooth payoffs push the population away from the mix when it starts nearby. Whether smooth pairs approach the kinked case in the limit is not established. For the kinked case at 0.8, a two-generation cycle is proved to exist but is not shown to attract. The wider region of cycling was computed numerically.

When modelling loses while rare and pays once common. With payoffs that vary without jumps across the range, the balanced mix becomes an unstable tipping point. From any start above it, the population goes all the way to modellers. From any start below it, modellers die out.

When one type always does better. With payoffs that vary without jumps across the range, the population goes all the way to that type from every mixed start, or holds steady if neither does better.

The attention schema theories are about access and the self-model, not the felt experience and sentience this guide follows. Under them, the conscious share is the modellers’ share times their verdict. With no mutation, a stable mix in the first regime has a positive access and self-model verdict only if the modellers’ own verdict is positive. That mix is locally stable when payoffs vary without jumps at it and selection is below two thirds, and stable from every mixed start under the further conditions above. In the second regime, the conscious share depends on history: on whether the population started above or below the tipping point.

Part 9. What measurements of machines can and cannot tell us

Three channels, and training on human text

The model sorts machine measurements into three channels. None of them takes consciousness itself as an input:

  • Behaviour and self-report: what the system does and says, read out through its trained policy.
  • Direct inspection of mechanisms: looking at architecture and internal activity. This bypasses the policy and reads only the structural features.
  • Steering: perturbing internal states and seeing how behaviour changes. This is motivated by reports, from interested parties, of injecting concepts and steering emotion-like directions (Chandaria et al. 2026).

When a system comes from selection on its own or from design, all three channels are native: they reflect its own organisation, not imitated human text. Direct inspection counts as native under every route, imitation included. That is a property of the channel as the model defines it, since it reads only structure. In practice, pretraining on human text sets the very weights that inspection reads, and real probes are trained or labelled with human concepts. Whether any actual probe tells apart the organisations it needs to is an unvalidated empirical gap.

Under imitation, the channels that pass through the policy face a fork:

  • The collapse branch is the parrot (or anthropomimesis) reading. Here imitation training forces behaviour to the same human-like report for every organisation in the architecture class. A readout that never varies carries zero evidence. Given the imitation-collapse assumptions, the report changes neither the chance that the final verdict reaches a given level nor the expected value of that verdict. Steering on this branch depends on an extra assumption. Under readout collapse, the policy gives the human-like response whatever state is injected, for instance because post-training suppresses the readout, and steering updates nothing. Under readout transparency, the policy passes the injected state through, and steering is native. I adopt neither as a default and leave the fork explicit.
  • The functionalist branch follows Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini (2024). Here all three channels stay native even under imitation.

Native indicators can do one thing well. On any finite list of candidate organisations, indicators that tell apart organisations with different degree profiles fix each organisation’s degree under every theory. Two cautions apply. An indicator with finitely many readings cannot separate a continuum of degrees. And that a real probe separates the list is an empirical premise, not a proved theorem. Even with every degree fixed, the weighted verdict across theories stays strictly between the lowest and highest verdicts whenever two theories with some weight disagree.

When watching selection can move the weights on theories

Suppose we watch a population change over generations. The model gives exact conditions for when that moves the starting weights across theories:

  • With no direct bonus, nothing moves. On the epiphenomenalist’s branch, the population’s path does not depend on which theory is true. Neither does the chance of any machine observation. The updated weights then equal the starting weights, thresholds included. This holds under a finite starting spread over costs, benefits, pressure weights, persistence and starting state that does not depend on the theory, and with observations of nonzero chance. It was machine-checked. It follows from three commitments of the model, not from evidence: verdicts depend only on organisation and hardware; observation channels take no theory as input; and the bonus is the only route by which theory enters fitness.
  • With a bonus but unknown costs, no path refutes a theory outright. For any path and any theory, some adjusted cost function reproduces the same path exactly. The same adjusted costs reproduce the dynamics from every starting state. That adjusted cost may be negative and enters non-energy cost so persistence is untouched, which is why non-refutation holds when costs are unconstrained. So no observed path can logically rule out a theory. But an observer with a finite spread of belief over costs can still update. In a constructed example where one cost is equally likely to be zero or 0.5, a single step drives the weight on the true theory to one.
  • With a bonus and observed costs, task payoffs, pressure weights and persistence, theories can be told apart in principle. This compares theories with a fixed positive consciousness weight, positive selection strength and a positive weight on intrinsic fitness, up to full weight. It also keeps the payoff scale and the developer-and-market baseline fixed and requires a mutation rule whose effects can be reversed mathematically. Task payoffs must be measured separately from any bonus for consciousness, an empirical separation I leave open. For each type present at the start, subtract the rival theory’s final verdict from the true theory’s. One genuine selection step tells the theories apart exactly when those differences cannot all be obtained by multiplying the rival’s effective fitness values by the same number. If the starting state is unobserved under a finite starting spread of belief over it, two consecutive later snapshots are needed; one can fail. If this holds against every rival with weight, and the true theory had some starting weight, the updated weight on the true theory is one.

On the first point, whether a zero bonus is the epiphenomenalism that Chandaria et al. (2026) exclude depends on one’s metaphysics. On a dualist reading, where consciousness has no physical effects, it is that excluded view. On a physicalist reading, where consciousness’s effects just are the organisation’s effects, a zero bonus fits a discoverable mapping from organisation to experience.

Rows whose fitness is proportional across the types present are not separated. These include rows that coincide on the hardware at matching thresholds and gates: the rows that are zero on digital hardware, Seth’s biological naturalism with enactivism, CARBON with IONIC, and integrated information, EM and QM when they coincide.

Isn’t any difference between two theories’ verdicts enough to tell them apart? Not always. A difference that is not constant across types is enough in three cases: when degrees are just zero or one; in continuous time with constant persistence; and when the differences on two types have opposite signs or one is zero. In general graded, generation-by-generation dynamics, it is not enough. In the technical paper, an example with two types has true-theory verdicts of three-fifths and two-fifths while the rival’s are both zero. The difference is not constant. Yet the true theory’s fitness values are exactly five-fourths of the rival’s, so selection produces the same next state under both and the weights do not move.

Two further conditions apply. The observed step must be a genuine selection step, not one that spans a developer’s post-training run. If post-training changes the structure, use measurements taken before the change to describe the earlier organisation and measurements taken after it to describe the later one. And learning fails sharply in two cases: when two theories differ by a constant across a uniform population with equal base fitness, and when a lineage of either type is equally likely to switch types or stay the same, so mutation produces the same mix regardless of the prior fitness differences.

No indicator reads the threshold

Even when native indicators fix every degree on the list, they read only structure. They never read a theory’s threshold, so a credence about a single system depends on that unmeasured threshold. The starting probabilities of those possible cutoffs are assigned within each theory. With the threshold independent of the readout, two credences follow once degrees are fixed:

  • The expected-degree credence is the choice I use. Within each theory, average the final verdicts over its possible cutoffs using their starting weights. If two cutoffs have equal weight, for example, take half the verdict under each. Then combine the theories using their starting weights. The result depends on the gate form.
  • The gate-open credence is the precautionary dissent: any system past the gate counts in full. For positive degrees, the two forms agree except at the exact shared cutoff, where the step gate is open and the ramp gate is shut.

Can selection data find a threshold that static indicators cannot? With a positive bonus, selection paths bear on two threshold versions of a theory only through the full conditions above. Costs, task payoffs, pressure weights and persistence must be observed, on a genuine selection step whose starting population straddles the threshold. This conclusion is argued in prose, not separately machine-checked.

Part 10. How the model sits beside Assessing AI Consciousness

Chandaria et al. (2026), Assessing AI Consciousness, set the philosophical “hard problem” aside in favour of a “mapping problem.” They assume “a discoverable, lawlike mapping” from a system’s examinable properties to its experience, specifically “information-bearing contents of consciousness.” That assumption excludes several views, including “epiphenomenalism, on which experiences have no effects and so leave no evidence by which the mapping could be established” (Chandaria et al. 2026).

They organise theories across five levels:

  1. behaviour;
  2. computational function, where global workspace, higher-order, recurrent processing, attention schema and predictive theories sit;
  3. intrinsic causal structure, home of integrated information theory;
  4. the organism, including Searle and Seth;
  5. organism and environment, including enactivism.

A level counts only if its properties “are not expressible at the adjacent coarser level.” Substrate requirements act as constraints that cut across levels and can veto. Indicators are sorted three ways: inapplicable, applicable but confounded (receiving an “anthropomimesis discount”), and applicable and valid. Chandaria and colleagues start with probabilities for which level matters. At each level, they compare how likely a reading is if the system is conscious with how likely it is otherwise. An inapplicable indicator gives neither possibility more weight. A reading that may partly reflect imitation is discounted, and a valid reading is used without that discount. They combine the probabilities of consciousness for the different levels using their starting probabilities for those levels. They issue no verdict on current systems, stressing that assessments “take the form of aggregated probabilities rather than verdicts” and that their range for large language models “should not be interpreted as an empirically established range” (Chandaria et al. 2026).

Four exact comparisons connect their framework to the model:

  1. Static evidence and theory weights. Their aggregation assumes that indicator evidence says nothing about which level is critical, “a modelling choice reflecting that theoretical credences are set by philosophical argument rather than system-level evidence” (Chandaria et al. 2026). A result for a single snapshot of a system, the snapshot lemma, makes the same modelling choice. It takes the independence as a hypothesis and does not derive it. In their framework, the assumption that indicator evidence leaves the starting level weights unchanged is paired with another. Once the readings are known, which level is critical does not change the probability of consciousness at any given level. In my snapshot result, I assume the true theory is independent of both the system’s organisation and its readings. Along selection paths with no bonus, by contrast, unchanged weights follow from the model’s three commitments under Part 9’s theory-free starting spread and nonzero chance, and Part 9 says when paths with a bonus move them. Their static rule thus matches the no-bonus result by stipulation. It is a noisy single-system analogue of the result on native indicators. A constant readout on the collapse branch carries no evidence, as does their inapplicable category.
  2. Families versus levels. Their levels 2 and 4, and the self-producing enactive slice of level 5, fit the model’s features and hardware with minor work. Level 3 appears through integrated information, EM and QM, and through readings of global workspace and recurrent processing theories when the optional integration switch is on. Only level 1—analytic behaviourism, where behaviour (the policy alone) is enough for consciousness—cannot be a row. The model restricts every theory to structure and keeps the policy out by construction.
  3. How far the anchor reaches. The model anchors direct observation and updates of that evidence in human report, as in their worked example of a human case. They draw level credences partly across species and ask for validation on mammals, birds and cephalopods.
  4. Extending indicators outward, population by population. They treat this programme of Bayne et al. (2024) and Peters (2026) as complementary, applying it to animals and borderline biological cases. As researchers try indicators on new populations, they use which markers tend to occur together to refine both the tests and their understanding of what counts as consciousness. The programme assumes that how likely an indicator reading is varies with the candidate theory. In the model, however, under Part 9’s theory-free starting spread and nonzero chance, a machine observation’s probability varies with the theory only when a consciousness bonus shifts which organisations evolve—because for any fixed organisation, the observation channels do not depend on the theory.

Part 11. Welfare counts only where a theory says the system feels, and how many subjects there are is left open

In When Is a Machine an Actor?, what a system is after is defined by consistent choices across the situations under consideration. Those choices provide a starting score for how it ranks outcomes. Any internal state that changes choices, such as the emotion-like steering reported by Chandaria et al. (2026), is folded into the situation before consistency is tested. That way, a choice that shifts with internal state is not misread as incoherence.

On the sentient branch, the theories whose target includes sentience are Solms’s theory, Seth’s biological naturalism, enactivism, CARBON and IONIC. Here the model adds welfare to the actor’s objective. The objective equals the baseline utility plus a welfare term. That term is welfare scaled by a non-negative weight and by a sentience weight: the starting weights of those five theories, each times its verdict on the system. The starting weights, the welfare function and proportional weighting are inputs to the model, not findings.

A theory of felt experience without the sentience tag contributes nothing to the sentience weight, for a human or a machine. The model gives no sentience verdict for it. The four hybrid sentience theories exclude digital hardware. So on digital hardware, the sentience weight comes from Solms’s theory alone: one seventh of the role family’s weight, times its expected verdict across its threshold split.

Two features shape how welfare enters:

  • Proportional versus all-or-nothing weighting. The sentience weight scales each sentient theory’s weight by its gated verdict. The precautionary dissent counts a sentient theory’s full starting weight once its gate opens. Choosing between them is a moral choice, not a theorem. On the step gate, the sentience weight jumps at any positive threshold, so a small change in architecture can shift the objective abruptly. The size of a theory’s jump is its starting weight times its degree at the cutoff. On the ramp gate, for a threshold below one, it rises smoothly from zero.
  • How many subjects? Total welfare is a sum over sentient subjects. The actor is the deployed lineage. The number of moral patients, or subjects whose welfare can count, remains open. Chandaria et al. (2026), after Chalmers, Beckmann and Butlin, note that a deployed model may be “potentially many” moral patients. The model leaves the count open across three readings: one subject per lineage, per running copy, or per conversation or episode.

Restricting welfare to the sentient branch lines up with one side of a philosophical dispute. List (2018) suggests that a capacity for felt experience is necessary for a being to matter morally in its own right. Long, Sebo et al. (2024) hold open a route through agency. The model excludes that route by construction. So a non-sentient system with consistent aims and a self-model gets zero welfare weight, even if rational agency alone confers moral standing.

Korsgaard (2008, 2018) also separates instrumental conduct from having a good of one’s own. In her texts, five levels are distinguished:

  • agency at all, needing a conscious organism guided by valenced representation;
  • purposes of one’s own, needing nothing beyond animal agency, since the antelope’s purpose “is her own”;
  • a good of one’s own, needing valenced consciousness, which is sentience;
  • acting for a reason and autonomy, needing rational self-consciousness, or “reflective distance”;
  • moral agency, needing the same rational self-consciousness.

Korsgaard (2018) asks: “[k]nives and cars are not guided by conscious valenced experiences, of course, but why exactly is that so important to having a final good?” Her answer is that “it is only creatures who are capable of being pleased and pained by things that are good- or bad-for them in the functional sense who have a final good.” She also writes that “it is conscious beings who have selves, or rather are selves, that make moral claims on us” (Korsgaard 2018). What a system is after, in the model, does not require a self-model. Her condition for a good of one’s own matches placing welfare on the sentient branch.

I recommend no policy here. Welfare protections and governance measures that depend on reading a system’s aims and moral status are examined in Measures That Depend on Ends.

Part 12. Under the model’s conditions, seven points follow for evaluating machines

For tests of real systems, the evidence results give seven points:

  1. No bonus, no update. By the model’s commitments, no path without a direct bonus moves the starting weights, under the stated conditions. Neither does a single snapshot of one system, under the snapshot lemma’s independence hypothesis.
  2. The imitation fork. Under imitation, behaviour carries zero evidence on the collapse branch and is native on the functionalist branch.
  3. Inspection is conditional. Direct inspection fixes degrees only on a finite list it is assumed to tell apart. The weighted verdict across theories stays strictly between their verdicts when theories with weight disagree.
  4. Steering and post-training. Under imitation, steering updates nothing under readout collapse and is native under readout transparency. Whether post-training moves only the policy or the structure remains open.
  5. Identifying theories in principle requires a bonus. With a bonus, paths can move the weights under a finite spread of belief over costs. The finite-cost example in Part 9 shows that in some cases, a step can bring the weight of the true theory to one even with unknown costs. The separate identification result gives conditions that guarantee that outcome with observed costs and payoffs. No current machine regime is shown to meet those conditions. Rows with proportional fitness are not separated.
  6. Indicators do not read thresholds. Credence about a single system depends on the threshold weights and the gate form.
  7. Finding thresholds from selection data is argued in prose, not separately machine-checked. It goes only through the full conditions of Part 9, on a genuine selection step whose starting population straddles the threshold.

How does machine consciousness bear on the conflict mechanisms of The War Baseline? By a lemma in When Is a Machine an Actor? (which concerns conflict outcomes rather than consciousness tests or Part 11’s welfare term), consciousness changes nothing in strategic conflict unless it changes a payoff—for instance through Frank’s commitment channels or through felt quality shifting a punishment penalty.

I neither rank nor recommend governance measures here. Measures that govern AI without any reading of a system’s aims or consciousness are developed in Measures That Need No Reading of Ends, my paper on governing AI without asking what it wants. Measures that depend on aims and welfare are examined in Measures That Depend on Ends.

Part 13. Controlled experiments could prove the premises wrong

No current observation settles which theory is true. The premises of the four dynamic results, though, are empirically falsifiable in controlled machine experiments:

  • The workspace result. In evolutionary runs where network architecture itself can vary across generations, global broadcast should become more common when tasks demand pooling information from several sources across delays. It should fail to spread when a non-broadcast architecture matches broadcast on such tasks, which would falsify the pooling premise, or when broadcast’s overhead exceeds its weighted gain. No training regime reviewed in the technical paper evolves architecture end to end. Self-play populations such as the hide-and-seek agents (Baker et al. 2020) can be inspected for emergent workspace-like bottlenecks.
  • The valence result. A population with nothing to lose that still evolves a costly valence trade-off would falsify the first valence result. By that result, such spread needs either a direct task benefit of valence that the model leaves out, or a bonus for feeling larger than its cost. Conversely, where persistence depends on regulating an essential variable through valence and the survival condition holds, valence spreads even with no bonus. The stock-and-reaper rule of Part 5 is a proposed test setting, not an outcome the model proves. Tierra’s reaper makes persistence depend on organisation (Ray 1991). Whether that dependence runs through valence is decided for no named system.
  • The social result. Where the payoff to modelling others and one’s own attention falls as modellers become more common, the modeller share should settle at the balanced mix from starts near it. This needs no mutation, payoffs that vary without jumps at the mix, the regime’s conditions, and selection below two thirds. Under the attention-schema theories, the conscious share would settle at the mix times the verdict. Settling at all or none falsifies the premise. With no mutation, where modelling loses while rare and pays once common and payoffs vary without jumps across the range, the population should split to all above the tipping point and none below.
  • The imitation fork. Holding pretraining text fixed, removing recurrence or broadcast should leave behaviour unchanged under imitation collapse. It should change behaviour on the functionalist branch if behaviour tells the tested organisations apart, and change inspection on both branches if inspection does. But removal degrades capability and may push the system outside the collapse branch’s architecture class. So the test is not yet runnable as stated and needs controls that are still unspecified. Under imitation collapse, steering can test whether the policy passes internal changes through to behaviour, provided it can distinguish the organisations being compared. Telling two theories apart by selection needs the full conditions of Part 9.

Part 14. What the model does not show

Here I state in one place what the model does not capture, has not measured and cannot claim.

Earlier false statements stay on record. The model was formalised and checked by the proof checker in a file of 236 theorems, 46 lemmas and 58 examples, in 5,317 lines, with no proof placeholders in the file. The checks were replayed on 16 November 2026. The report on the most recently added result lists only the usual assumptions of the proof system. Earlier versions of the specification made statements that the checker refuted, and the record keeps them:

  • An earlier bound on fitness used the consciousness weight alone instead of the weight times the verdict. It allowed negative fitness.
  • An earlier claim said that in discrete generations only mixed populations separate theories. It was proved false; the claim holds instead in continuous time.
  • An earlier claim said theories could be identified from a single later snapshot when the starting state is unobserved. A proved counterexample refuted it, and two later snapshots forming one observed step are needed.

Two types only, and some items not machine-checked. The baseline comparisons with and without mutation, the valence comparisons, and the social-modelling results are proved for two types. So is the appendix’s formula for the chance that one type takes over a finite population. That finite-population formula is not applied in the main selection results. The full conclusion of the something-to-lose result was checked when degrees are zero or one, and argued in the graded case from checked facts. The separate equal-survival result’s comparison of the added cost with the change in the consciousness contribution was machine-checked even for graded degrees. Six items in the closing table are neither checked nor refuted:

  • which results only rescale when consciousness is graded and which change at a threshold (argued);
  • extending the small-share bound for valence to many types (not established; a conjecture);
  • whether smooth payoffs approach the kinked case in the social result (not established, and not in the checker);
  • the wider cycling region beyond the proved two-generation cycle (computed numerically);
  • that this finite-population takeover chance satisfies the random birth-and-death process’s step-by-step equations (proved by hand; uniqueness and the formula are machine-checked);
  • that selection data identify a threshold only through Part 9’s full observation conditions where the disputed organisations straddle it (argued).

The history-dependent stock-and-reaper form of persistence is left unformalised. So is a model in which a stake held by single copies changes the number of sentient subjects.

No empirical source for thresholds, gates or degrees. The threshold classes, the shared cutoff and the two gate forms are modelling choices with no empirical source. The record of degrees has no empirical calibration. No degree is assigned to any system, and none may be read off any named model.

No named system assessed or placed on a branch. For no named system does the model decide its structural features, or which post-training, imitation or steering branch it is on. It leaves unsettled how a lineage that is both trained by imitation and selected across generations in a market should have its path and its observation route treated together. And because the higher-order row reads the general self-model feature, the prediction and social pressures raise its degree, and its verdict above the cutoff, by routes higher-order theories do not name.

Provisional rows and unread sources. Four hardware-constraint rows—CARBON, IONIC, EM and QM (two hybrid and two substrate)—are provisional. McFadden (2020) was read in full for EM. CARBON, IONIC and QM still rely on secondary reporting in Chandaria et al. (2026), and the primary works by Hameroff and Penrose (2014), Neven et al. (2024) and Lane (2022) are unread. So all counts are given with and without these rows. The technical paper’s declarations say how AI models were used in the work, including that a Claude model assessed Chandaria et al. (2026), whose primary evidence concerns Claude models.

Scope limits. The fixed-weight comparisons, and the mutation-free social analysis, do not establish their outcomes for the illustrative deployment profile. In that profile, mutation is positive and pressure weights depend on the state of the population. The model takes a finite list of candidate organisations as given. It cannot evolve new organisations from scratch. It cannot settle whether any current regime is a paradigm Darwinian population, or whether any meets the observation conditions for telling theories apart. It cannot say which theory is true.

Other questions are also left open. Separating a bonus for consciousness from task payoffs is an open problem. Shutdown counts alone cannot show whether valence or stake, rather than developer choices, changes persistence. Channels that are only partly confounded are not modelled. Theories of felt experience without the sentience tag contribute nothing to the sentience weight. And with no direct bonus, under the conditions of Part 2, the weighted verdict across theories remains an average over theories, not a finding.

What would change the answer. Within the model, the answers turn on a few things that could, in principle, be observed. Do costs, task payoffs and persistence become measurable? Does consciousness carry a direct fitness effect? Does persistence run through a lineage’s own valence or stake? Do real probes tell candidate organisations apart? Part 13 lists the experiments that could falsify each dynamic premise.

Part 15. What to take away

The baseline comparisons put two types on fixed hardware, hold pressure weights fixed, and give some weight to selection and intrinsic fitness. If one type’s effective fitness after accounting for survival is higher, it takes over from a mixed start when there is no mutation, an idealisation outside the deployment dial. With equal two-way mutation at a positive rate below one half, the population settles into a unique mix, and that type is the majority. Its share approaches the whole population as the mutation rate goes to zero. The illustrative deployment profile instead has changing pressure weights and ongoing mutation. At equal survival, the comparison comes down to the gain in weighted benefits, minus the extra cost, plus any change in the direct contribution from consciousness. When both types are below a theory’s threshold, consciousness makes no difference to their relative fitness. With no task payoff depending on valence, costly valence is selected away at equal persistence when its consciousness bonus is below its extra cost, yet spreads for any non-negative consciousness weight within the model’s bound when its survival ratio beats its zero-bonus effective-fitness disadvantage. With no mutation, frequency-dependent social pressure can hold a population at a mix or make the outcome depend on where it started.

Ten of seventeen theories, or six of thirteen without the provisional ones, rule out conventional digital hardware. That is a property of the roster, not a measurement. With no direct bonus for consciousness, the model’s own commitments mean that watching selection leaves the weights on theories unchanged, under its conditions. Under imitation collapse, self-report carries no evidence. No indicator reads a threshold.

By construction in When Is a Machine an Actor?, a lineage need not feel to be the strategic actor that The War Baseline models. Whether a machine can feel, whether it lives, and whether its aims are truly its own remain open. Those questions decide what we owe it and how far the protections examined in Measures That Depend on Ends reach.
