In brief

A worried student asked whether AI will eliminate humanity, and whether she should change her plans. I do not give her a measured or pooled risk number. There are no comparable past events to count, and published figures are beliefs about different questions over different spans of time, so they cannot be pooled. There is also no defensible number for how much the proposed measures would lower the risk. A machine smarter than any human brain could be would count as superintelligence. The series models how the transition to such a machine could go badly or go better and asks what could make the better paths likelier. What the models establish is conditions within models under which a mechanism helps, not a ranking of mechanisms by net risk, and my answer to the student comes at the end as counsel, a judgment rather than a finding.

Part 1. Why I do not give the student a measured or pooled number

A worried student asked whether artificial intelligence will eliminate humanity, and whether to change course in her own plans. In September 2026 an Anthropic researcher, Jacob Coxon, quit. He warned that the labs building AI are “racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives” (Bellan 2026). Here, superintelligence means a machine smarter than any human brain could be.

News reports put the chance at 10%. That figure is a degree of belief, not a measurement. As Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor note, there is no set of comparable past events to count from, “as it is an event like no other” (Narayanan and Kapoor 2024).

The same 10% also comes, by a separate route, from two AI Impacts surveys of machine-learning researchers, run in 2022 and 2023 (Grace et al. 2024). Respondents were asked whether “human inability to control” future AI would cause extinction or a similarly permanent loss of human power. Their median answer was 10% in both years. When the question said “future AI advances,” the median was 5%. No respondent saw both wordings, and about one in seven replied.

The 10% in the September 2026 reports was not Coxon’s. It was the personal estimate of his Anthropic colleague Evan Hubinger, “>10% within the next decade” (Maxwell 2026; Bellan 2026). Toby Ord’s “1 in 10” for what he calls unaligned AI covers a century (Ord 2024). These figures answer different questions over different spans of time, so they cannot be pooled as estimates of one common risk.

So I do not answer her with a measured or pooled number. Limits, and an Answer, my closing essay in this series, also notes that no defensible number says how much the proposed measures would lower the risk. The answer it gives her is counsel, a judgment rather than a finding. Instead of a number, the series builds general models of how the transition could go badly and how it could go better. The aim is to ask which mechanisms could make the better paths likelier.

Part 2. Fifteen papers, each taking one question

Seventeen papers do this work: this Introduction, fifteen papers that each own one question, and the closing essay. They model the near-term transition in cyber security and in biology and health. They ask when a machine may be treated as an actor, and they model conflict between humans and one or more superintelligences. They set out an untested conjecture: that what a mind wants (the papers’ “ends”) shapes the conflict it enters. The record of exceptional human minds suggested the conjecture and illustrates it, and a design for testing it comes with it. Then come governance and training measures while humans still hold control. Last come the limits of all of it. Each paper has a plain-language guide like this one.

“AI” names four different actors, and the series keeps them apart: - a single autonomous machine dealing with humanity; - labs and states racing to build one; - AI systems used by people as tools; - many AI systems dealing with one another.

Humanity is not one actor either. Satiation Is Not Enough models it as communities, any one of which can build a rival machine. The list under “Where to read more” says which paper to open for which question.

Part 3. In the cyber model, speeding up can lower or raise harm around the peak

Should we run faster through the transition, or hold dangerous capability back? Running Through the Rain, on speed and secrecy in the AI cyber transition, studies both arguments. It takes the case where cyber risk peaks on the way, because code-breaking AI helps attackers before it helps defenders. A peak can arise when new flaws outpace their removal and later fall behind. Whether AI creates or moves the peak is not modelled.

Danger there comes in two kinds. A speed-set danger rises with how fast capability advances. A level-set danger tracks how much capability has been reached. Deployment here means putting the capability to work for defence (the technical paper’s “adoption”).

In the model, speeding up can lower the harm around the peak when deployment runs at least partly alongside capability development and capability development, not deployment, sets how long the transition lasts. Past a turning point (the “kink”), deployment sets that length instead. There, with deployment at least partly alongside and its time fixed, speeding up raises harm around the peak. That holds under a speed-set danger. It also holds under a level-set danger whose value at the end of the path exceeds its average along it. Under a speed-set danger, speeding up past the turning point lowers harm only if deployment time falls with speed faster, in proportion, than the danger rises.

Holding back in secret has firm limits in that model. A secret hold saves a protected operator only a limited amount of exposure time. The saving is at most the shorter of two spans: the secrecy window and that operator’s own deployment time. It works only against attackers without access. It delays the search for unfound flaws by at most the window. That makes safeguarded release its benchmark: release with protections that delay attackers’ access while defenders use the capability. Where real speeds sit, how danger and deployment respond, and how long a window lasts are all unmeasured, and no ranking of interventions follows.

Within the health model’s conditions, the balance is undetermined

Running Toward the Clinic, on speed, biological risk and health, builds on that paper, which stands alone. It asks whether faster AI progress helps health once three harms are counted in healthy life-years: biological misuse, laboratory releases and hospital cyberattacks. That also raises the question of whether the narrow case for speed survives. It works through open modelling choices, chief among them whether danger tracks the level of capability or its speed. Within its stated conditions, the draft finds that whether faster progress helps on balance is undetermined.

Some results hold across those choices. One is that faster progress raises misuse harm once dangerous capability exists and no defence closes it. Its one measured source of harm, deaths from hospital ransomware, is estimated only within a range. Misuse is unmeasured, and I adopt no rule for deciding. Both papers treat AI as capability in human hands.

Part 4. When the model can treat a machine as an actor

The study of war treats states as single rational actors. Each chooses the best means to its ends, given its beliefs. Can that method carry over to a superintelligent machine? When Is a Machine an Actor? asks two prior questions. When does consistent behavior justify treating a machine as a strategic actor with ends of its own? And what counts as one actor across copies and retrained versions?

It examines five views of whether a machine has to be conscious or alive to make strategic choices. Its formal model defines ends by behavior, on the machine’s policy: its rule for acting in each situation. The draft finds that for theories of consciousness that depend only on a system’s structure, this test of ends does not wait on felt consciousness. In the model, that holds by construction for those theories.

Here is how it works. Suppose the score used to select the machine depends only on what happens. In a limited set of test situations, suppose no two outcomes in the same situation get the same score. The rule that gets the highest scores then gives consistent choices in those situations, and the scoring system explains those choices. Two questions stay open. Consistency in untested strategic situations remains an assumption. Whether the record of human-staffed states carries over to a machine is a separate empirical question. As a separate benchmark, when tested choices among a limited set of outcomes never put an outcome ahead of itself through a chain of preferences, they fix a strict ranking only on the comparisons those choices imply. Two policies that agree on the tested situations and share one structure do equally well under selection when cost depends only on structure and tested choices.

Copies and versions are settled by convention. Deployed copies of one version count as one actor when together they cover all the situations in the model and their combined choices never put an outcome ahead of itself through a chain of preferences. Copies whose behavior drifts or that face some of the same options can fail that test. Counting each new or retrained version as a new actor is a further convention, which selection theory does not derive. So if a later model follows the same machine through retraining, it has to assume that the machine’s ends carry over.

Could It Feel? asks what this leaves open: can a machine lineage come to feel, and could we tell? The answer bears on what is owed to a machine and on how far analogies from human minds reach. The draft models selection on artificial lineages across software generations on one class of hardware. That selection is likened to the selection that may have shaped mammalian nervous systems. Starting beliefs are spread across seventeen theories of consciousness.

By the model’s commitments, no observed course of selection shifts beliefs about which theory holds when three conditions hold: consciousness has no direct effect on fitness, starting beliefs about the model’s settings and starting state cover finitely many possibilities and do not depend on the theory, and the observed course of selection has some chance of occurring under the model. And when training on human text collapses self-report into imitation (“imitation collapse”), an unchanging self-report leaves those beliefs unchanged too. Conclusions about feeling still average across theories, giving more weight to theories the model treats as more plausible. Whether a machine can feel remains an open empirical question.

Part 5. With a power-seeking machine, the war baseline is bleak

The War Baseline asks what models of how opponents reach a deal or go to war predict under three stated premises: - the machine is a single rational actor that maximizes power and how long it lasts; - once it is self-protected, so that humans cannot substantially damage the inputs it fights over, it fights far more cheaply than humans do; - it wants compute (computing power), energy and matter, which can be divided, handed over and piled up.

Call a machine dominant when it can defeat every human-side check, not merely when it is smarter than any human. Once it is dominant, it cannot credibly promise costly restraint. That is the machine’s commitment problem. Humanity, for its part, cannot credibly promise not to build a profitable rival. That is humanity’s commitment problem. War to head off the shift into dominance can start only if the coming change in power, after giving less weight to changes farther in the future, is larger than both sides’ costs of fighting for one period added together.

How the inputs change hands matters. Deals that hand them over in one lump fail because the receiver cannot commit to stop, in an unpublished model Smith and I built (Smith and Stam 2001). On Fearon’s smooth-slicing path, fine slices avoid war while dispossessing humans (Fearon 1996). In The War Baseline, I also give a hand-argued reading of Powell (2006) for transfers in separate steps. On that reading, war comes back for sides that give enough weight to the future when a step shifts the odds of winning by more than the combined per-period fighting costs, which are nearly the human cost. Which kind of transfer occurs remains open.

The baseline also marks which conclusions depend on the machine maximizing power. Four do: how wide the room for a deal is, whether the machine stops at a secure core (the “heartland exit”), whether it can be satisfied, and whether relative position governs the bargain. Three do not: whether wants that value continued existence still create a security motive to expand, whether humans build a rival where it pays, and whether resources pile up. Later papers build on that division.

Open disagreement, free fighting and rival machines

Disagreement Without Hidden Information asks whether rational actors who hide nothing and know each other’s beliefs can still fight. Suppose they share the same starting beliefs (a “common prior”) and reason fully rationally. Then disclosure and credible commitment remove rational war. With different starting beliefs, open actors can still fight. Fey and Ramsay (2007) prove, within their class of models, that boundedly rational reasoning, where actors process information imperfectly, from shared starting beliefs has the same implications for strategic choices as fully rational reasoning from different starting beliefs. My comparison there is between shared starting beliefs with fully rational reasoning and different starting beliefs once actors are open and can credibly commit: under its stated conditions, those two accounts differ before dominance on when war starts among racing builders, and on racing itself.

On an argued reading there, training choices made before any data may partly set how a machine updates its beliefs. The net size of the risk is undetermined. What changes is that leftover risk shifts from secrets to open disagreement. All the conclusions that depend on a superintelligence’s beliefs assume the actors know those beliefs. The analysis does not establish those conclusions for one of the cases it distinguishes. The settlements analysed work among human parties before dominance.

Fighting for Free corrects a rule in an earlier model Smith and I built (Smith and Stam 2004). In that article’s published example, accepting any offer at least as good as what the responder expects to get by fighting to the end is not a best response. In equilibrium, where each side’s strategy is a best response to the other’s, the responder weighs an offer against its value of rejecting. The model is then extended to a machine that fights at zero cost against humans who pay a positive cost per battle. Suppose starting beliefs come from the beta family (a standard family of probability curves between zero and one), the prize is positive, and the combined cost per battle is positive. Then every subgame-perfect equilibrium, meaning one whose strategies stay best responses at every point in the game, settles by a finite deadline unless the war is decided first. The baseline calculations track how often wars settle. The overall rate counts surrenders as settlements. Under some offers from the machine, the rate that excludes surrenders moves in a different direction from the overall rate. In all these models the loser survives by construction, because a game over contested positions has no move that removes the other player.

Much writing, as noted in Takeoff and Emergence, expects the first project to begin improving itself in ways that let it make further improvements to end up alone. That paper asks when more than one superintelligence can instead emerge and stay relevant. A follower must avoid being outclassed in capability. It must also survive a window: the time between the leader reaching the point where its share locks in and the follower reaching that same point. When advantage depends on the ratio of capabilities, steady compounding makes a lag decisive if and only if the lag spans enough doublings. Suppose the conditions governing a strike stay fixed and the amount of future share the follower can lock in jumps when it crosses the threshold. Then a slower takeoff leaves the follower closer to the leader and gives the leader more reason to strike during the window. So peaceful emergence of two machines can be confined to a middle band. Having several machines does not by itself settle human safety.

In the models of Many Machines, several superintelligent machines could protect, exploit or eliminate humans, and their number alone does not decide which. The models examine bargaining, deterrence, the risk of attack, and how far the machines can exert power. A bloc of machines whose wants have converged sets only a ceiling on humanity’s safe share. The ceiling rises with what humans can make a seizure cost. It falls as the bloc weighs human holdings more heavily against its own. So it is positive only to the extent that humans can make seizure costly, and it can be zero.

In one model, rival machines can protect a larger human share than a bloc could. Assume all-or-nothing seizure, no prize for rank, and no joint seizure. Then rivals guard a human share above a bloc’s ceiling only when all three of these hold: - neither rival is hopeless in a general war between them; - each weighs its rival’s gains more heavily than humanity’s; - the share is large enough to be worth defending, yet too small to tempt general war.

Cheap war between rivals can lower the ceiling. These are bounds on safe shares, not predictions of the deal chosen. In that paper’s conclusion, everything turns on three things: what the machines want, the cost and technology of conflict between them, and what humans can defend or withhold. Disagreement Without Hidden Information and Fighting for Free take up questions from The War Baseline. Takeoff and Emergence and Many Machines ask their own questions in separate models.

Part 6. In the models, a machine that wants only so much may still not stop

What if the machine does not want ever more? Satiation Is Not Enough works in a one-sided conflict model. Call a want satiable when what it yields each period hits a ceiling; suppose also that it drops to zero on defeat. Even then, satiation alone does not make stopping best if the machine faces an ongoing strike risk that more holdings reduce. Against a strike risk taken as given, the gain from one more holding has two parts. Satiation removes the part from what the machine wants. It leaves the security part untouched.

Satiation is enough, at every fighting cost, when more holdings do not lower the strike risk. Now suppose holdings do lower a strike risk that is not certain, and total domination lowers it further. Then, below some positive attack cost, stopping short of domination is not the machine’s best choice in the model.

In that model, humanity is a set of communities with two ways to strike, and the two fail differently as communities multiply. Pulling a shared plug needs every community, so any imperfect, costly plug coalition eventually dissolves. Building a rival needs only one community. So when each community’s chance of trying is fixed, independent rival risk persists and blocks cheap stopping.

Mutual restraint can still be stable. Take an equilibrium where choices depend only on current holdings and both sides hold back at the satiation level. There, zero strike risk leaves the satiated machine no gain from expanding. Such an equilibrium exists if and only if no community profits from striking alone at that level. This holds even when play at higher levels includes credible strikes.

If wanting less is not enough, does the kind of thing a mind wants matter? Ends, Rank and Conflict varies what a machine values beyond power and duration. It separates three ideas. Assigned rank is a position in an ordering, given a comparison group and a criterion. Positionality is having wants whose value depends on standing relative to others. Rank competition means competing for a place in that ordering. It also shows that the reviewed observational studies of states establish associations and case readings, not tested mechanisms.

It then sets out eight dimensions of wants for an untested conjecture. Two concern the good a want is about, four the want itself, and two the inputs and how they convert. The conjecture links wants to conquest and coercive killing. Whether recognition can satisfy a desire for standing affects that link. It also links them to genocide or expulsion, and to dispossession when exchange fails. Satiable wants it links to a wider set of beliefs under which a deal exists. Biographies illustrate the dimensions. The record illustrates the link from wants to violence rather than deriving it.

Its formal results are narrower and cover rival stakes (stakes one side cannot hold without depriving the other) in one kind of war. In models that examine whether a given offer is acceptable, caring about rank over shares of the prize acts like lower war costs. Valuing a rival’s losses, with rank over net outcomes, can leave no deal possible. That analysis also borrows the stopping model from Satiation Is Not Enough, with zero strike risk at domination. In that model, security keeps a satiated actor expanding below a positive attack cost when conquest lowers a positive strike risk.

A test that could fail, and what changing wants does

A Test the Conjecture Could Lose starts from a conditional. Suppose what an actor wants shapes its conflicts, and training objectives fix what a machine learns to want. Then choosing and bounding an objective is a lever on conflict risk. There I set out a design written for pre-registration, meaning a plan to put the tests on record before running them. The design aims to avoid circularity and avoid rediscovering the cases that suggested the conjecture. It has a historical side and a sandbox where machine agents trained by reward are tested on situations like those they trained on. Nothing has been run.

Each of its six hypotheses has its own condition for losing. Each can lose in at least one part of the study, though not in every part. The conjecture as a whole has no single rule for losing. - The human side draws leaders from an unselected roster. It classifies the wants they stated before taking office separately from what they acquired and the violence they used. - The first hypothesis compares heirs who did not choose office, holding fixed the threat they faced on taking power. It tests a contrast in whether their stated wants were positional. That comparison is an association, causal only under an untestable assumption. With the number of cases expected from the archives, the design uses a fallback test that can reject the hypothesis but cannot support it. - The second hypothesis, that wants stated before office improve prediction of the type of violence, cannot be rejected in one rare part of the historical study. - The machine side varies what the agents are rewarded for, whether their objectives have limits, how scarce resources are, how readily resources can be turned into other things, and the scope for taking resources from other agents. Six fixed rules score each hypothesis as lost, supported, or “estimation”, a verdict that reports an estimate without supporting or rejecting.

A feasibility stage comes first.

Changing the Ends reruns the mechanisms of the war baseline with different wants. The model still treats the machine as a single rational actor. It sets aside the commitment problem during the transition and races among builders. Changing what the machine wants alters part of the baseline and leaves the rest intact. Wants that are not positional widen the room for a deal when a side values what its rival loses. Non-rival wants with non-rival inputs, things one side can have without depriving the other, leave more room. Even so, the bargaining models still assume the loser survives.

One case is a machine that values knowledge. Assume two things. What it learns from watching us starts above the cost of keeping us and then tends to zero. And leftover risk that we build a rival puts a floor under that cost. Then such a want gives reason to keep humans watched, not free, for a time.

After dominance, assume every other device protecting human claims to contested resources needs human enforcement, and no human output rises with humanity’s share. Then the machine’s own concern for those human claims is the only device that protects those claims, among those considered there and in Measures That Depend on Ends, my paper on training and governance aimed at what a machine wants. Neither human unity nor many machines guarantees safety.

Part 7. Most levers work only while humans still hold control

What can people do while they still hold control? The series sorts measures by what they inspect or act on. Measures That Need No Reading of Ends, on governing AI without asking what it wants, takes outputs, records, infrastructure and human parties. Measures That Depend on Ends takes internal states and training. Measures of the first kind can begin before anyone can read a machine’s wants.

Measures that need no reading of wants

Under the stated conditions in Measures That Need No Reading of Ends, sources of risk that domestic cyber rules leave untouched cap what the rules can achieve on their own. That analysis also gives conditional results on limits to steps in computing power among human rivals. It uses the definition of robustness from Measures That Depend on Ends. And it cites five candidate measures without screening them: making computing resources and training for the most advanced systems visible, reporting incidents, jointly evaluating what systems can do, making changes that can be undone, and setting rules against misuse.

The candidates come from a robustness screen that needs an unproved condition: every party whose participation is essential must expect enough benefit to cover its own cost. Measures That Depend on Ends defines that screen and supplies the five candidates. Being a candidate does not establish how much a measure lowers risk. On the account in Limits, and an Answer, the screen tests only two priors (starting beliefs fed into the test), the higher at 10 percent. Four candidates, all but compute visibility, would pass across priors only if the unproved condition held for every prior.

That leaves two gaps. Priors between the two are covered only by my assumption in that account that results move one way between them. Priors above 10 percent are untested. Making the computing resources and training for the most advanced systems visible does not pass the test while its effects on racing remain disputed. Its status as a candidate still depends on how it affects the race to build those systems.

Most direct levers over a machine hold only while it still depends on human infrastructure. Speed can also bind where machine agents act faster than people can review. The costs and benefits of the candidates are unmeasured, as are the costs of the interventions in Running Through the Rain. No lever is ranked above another.

Measures that depend on wants

Measures That Depend on Ends starts from an asymmetry: every lever humans hold over an advanced AI works only before it dominates. That is the period while humans still set its rewards and permissions, commit to the rules and can enforce prohibitions. Call it the window (the technical paper’s “human-enforcement window”). Within it, training is the one lever that bears on what the machine will want.

The review in that paper finds limits on every side. Whoever holds a model can strip off its filters. So refusal against those holders must live in the weights, the learned numbers inside the model. There, fine-tuning, or further training of the model, can undo it. So can removing the internal direction that carries refusal, a change called “directional ablation”. Current screens detect dispositions, not wants. Three restraint programs (quantilizers, attainable utility preservation and assistance games) restrain action under stated conditions without making wants satiable. Satiable goals remain an unproved conjecture.

Before dominance, a rising system can be paid in rules, voice and standing, not compute or autonomy. That works only for wants bounded against what force would win. Once the system could take everything, paying it requires satiable wants, which are unproved.

For designing rules within the window, I draw there on one model (Bergemann, Koh and Morris 2026), whose results are conditional. That model assumes capability can be hidden but not faked. This is the model’s assumption, not a finding. In their model: - tests of what a machine can do, used to decide whether to deploy it, do as well as full knowledge of the machine if the gap between its aims and the designer’s aims shrinks as capability rises; - the same tests do no better than one uniform limit if that gap grows with capability; - where any machine can claim any degree of agreement with the designer’s aims, one uniform limit is the best a designer can do; - under the best uniform limit, shrinking that gap on average and narrowing uncertainty about it make each other more valuable; - designed competition disciplines machines only if they do not collude.

My own calculation there extends their illustrative numbers to a possible loss of control. It compares two training choices: the one that is best while the limits hold, and an alternative. Relative to the alternative, the first choice’s extra loss if control fails is about four times its saving while control holds. Suppose the chance of losing control is the same under both choices. Then the limits-favored choice has the lower expected loss only when that chance is below about 0.20.

That figure comes from an illustrative comparison of two choices alone. It was computed but not checked by a proof-checking program. Such a program confirms that a conclusion follows from the stated assumptions, not that the assumptions hold in the world. Neither the 0.20 cutoff nor the 10 percent prior estimates risk. Their model does not cover the period after human control ends, which other papers of the series examine.

Above all, I argue in Measures That Depend on Ends that the eleven protective factors across the series do not fail independently. They share five causes: - deception; - one training process; - a fast capability jump; - weight release or theft; - human disunity.

Each cause can defeat several protections, or the evidence for them, at once. As a caveat, so can the protection package itself.

As an analogy only: several locks on a door feel safe together, unless one stolen key opens them all. Where a shared cause can fail every factor at once and the other failures are independent, multiplying the separate failure chances overstates safety. Limits, and an Answer adds that a shared cause alone does not guarantee this.

My untested judgment there has two parts. Combine protections that are exposed to different shared causes. And let states and labs settle early inside the window rather than wait to keep options open, with monitoring and pre-set triggers for renegotiation while human enforcement holds. Several of these results hold only inside the window, and some only under conditions we cannot yet check.

Part 8. What none of this claims, and what survives

Three cautions govern the series

First, the bargaining models of war were built for states, not machines. Every use of them for a machine is an argument by analogy. This covers the papers from The War Baseline through Ends, Rank and Conflict, and Changing the Ends, wherever they use war, bargaining, deterrence, stopping or related conflict models for machines. Their roles differ. Some analyse or rerun the war baseline. Fighting for Free uses “machine” for a side of the model marked out by its cost per battle. Others apply takeoff and power models, extend the war model Smith and I built, in which war moves step by step across contested positions (a “random walk”), to one-sided stopping, or reuse those models. Could It Feel? names a further analogy, to the selection that may have shaped mammalian nervous systems, and Limits, and an Answer assesses where the analogy from war and human minds to superintelligence holds and where it is thinnest.

Second, results can depend on which of the four actors a model represents. What holds for one actor, or for a humanity treated as one, need not hold for another. Third, the premises of every formal model in the series are a baseline, not a forecast.

The claims stand on different footings

Some claims rest on analogy. Some rest on convention, like the rule for when copies count as one actor. Others rest on argued readings, like the reading of Powell. The sorting of wants is an untested conjecture, and the test is a design not yet run. Some results are computations, as in Fighting for Free. Others are proved by hand or, where a paper says so, checked by a proof-checking program. Limits, and an Answer says this of small formal models of four of its prose sentences. Some conclusions are my judgment, and are labelled as such.

No model in the series says how long human control lasts. Key quantities are unmeasured, from how danger and deployment respond in Running Through the Rain to the costs and benefits of the candidate measures.

In the other papers in the series, I mark whose model or computation claim is whose. What a cited model shows is marked “in their model”. A citation identifies the source of a cited finding. What a model or computation of the series shows is marked “in our model” or “in the computation”. Earlier models I coauthored are attributed explicitly. Corrections or extensions belong to the series paper that makes them. Within those papers’ model and computation claims, my own inference or judgment is marked where it extends a cited result or could be read as one, and is otherwise unmarked. None of the papers has been refereed. Most state their argument in prose in the main text and put exact statements, computations or rules in appendices. Papers with formal results say which statements are checked by a proof-checking program, proved by hand, computed, conjectured or judged. Each paper ends with declarations of how it was made, including the part AI models played.

Where the analogy is thinnest

Limits, and an Answer turns these cautions on the argument itself. The sorting of wants is a conjecture built on a short, selected human record. The essay finds no source showing that satiable or non-positional goals can be trained or can survive a machine’s changes to itself. The measures carry no defensible number for how much they lower risk. The essay also assumes that failures on the machine side tend to occur together.

In that essay I judge that once a machine can defeat every human-side check, human-side safeguards can no longer be relied on. Under that condition, what is left is the bet on what the machine wants, and a conditional balance among ungoverned machines. Many Machines models safe-share bounds for that balance under stated assumptions, and no evidence tests it. The essay also gathers every measure the series proposes or weighs, with what each rests on. That inventory is in Section 10 and Appendix B. The essay also reports the findings and stated limits of the papers outside the war argument.

What survives is conditional

What the series establishes is conditions within models under which a mechanism helps. It does not rank mechanisms by net risk. The war baseline is bleak for a power-seeking machine. Non-rival wants with non-rival inputs leave more room for a deal. Satiation can make a machine’s promise to stop credible, in the case in Satiation Is Not Enough where more holdings do not lower the strike risk. Even so, the security problem can return from the human side when any one community can build a rival, if rival-building creates a strike risk that extra holdings can lower.

Limits, and an Answer asks which choices do not wait on a number. It returns to the robustness candidates, which remain untested above 10 percent and subject to the other conditions above. On my own judgment there, it also supports research on what training builds in. Then it returns to the student. It says why the models by themselves cannot answer her, and gives my answer as counsel rather than as a finding.

Part 9. What to take away

I do not give the student a measured or pooled risk number. There are no comparable past events to count, and the published figures cannot be pooled because they express beliefs about different questions over different spans of time. The series offers models instead, and what they establish is conditions within models under which a mechanism helps, not a ranking by net risk. With a power-seeking machine the war baseline is bleak, and what a machine wants can change part of that picture. Most levers humans hold work only while humans still hold control. The answer to the student comes at the end of the series, as counsel, not as a finding.
