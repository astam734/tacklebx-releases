This is the plain-language companion to the technical paper “Running Through the Rain: Speed, Secrecy and Risk in the AI Cyber Transition.” Everything here comes from that paper and its supplement. Where the paper is unsure, so is this guide.

If you have to cross a street in the rain, should you walk or run?

Run. Physicists have worked it out. Running cuts the time rain falls on your head. And you pass through the same curtain of rain in front of you at any speed. The one exception is a tailwind.1

Some people think AI and computer security work the same way. AI that can find and exploit flaws in software may help attackers first. Later it may help defenders, by fixing code and proving it correct. So, the argument goes, sprint through the dangerous middle.

The paper asks whether the rain picture holds. It finds two places where it breaks (Figure 1).

First, the far curb is built by other people. Safety arrives when the people who run software patch it, retire it or rewrite it. They do that on calendar time, not on AI’s schedule. Three separate records describe known patches and migration milestones (Figure 2):

  • One lab’s disclosure program reported 6,157 vulnerabilities disclosed across 591 open-source projects as of 2 October 2026. “To our knowledge,” the lab added, “516 of these have been patched.”2 That is the lab’s own dated count. It does not measure how fast fixes reach the computers that need them.
  • In June 2019 the U.S. Government Accountability Office named ten critical federal legacy systems most in need of modernization. As of February 2025, agencies reported three of the ten done.3
  • Native IPv6 access to Google first passed 50% on a single day, 28 March 2026, about 27 years after the December 1998 specification.4

Second, running thickens the rain. The paper’s models put attackers and defenders on one AI clock. Faster AI arms both at once.

From those two breaks comes the paper’s main result about speed, and it is narrow. In the model, going faster only adds risk once the time it takes to fix things, not AI’s pace, sets how long the danger lasts. That holds under conditions this guide spells out. Before that point, going faster can help.

Nobody has measured which side of that point we are on.

The paper also asks whether a firm should build the capability, hold it back and share it only with trusted defenders. And it asks what pushes firms to release what they hold. Those answers are conditional too. A new part asks whether life-saving benefits from the same AI change the speed answer (Part 6).

Part 1The question: should we rush through a risk peak?

The larger road is the one to artificial superintelligence, or ASI: AI that outperforms the best humans at nearly all thinking work.5 On that road, are there intermediate peaks in risk, stretches where danger climbs and then falls? If so, should we get through them faster?

The obvious candidate is the cyber transition, when AI can write and break code. The paper takes five questions:

  1. Is there a peak at all? Or does risk level off, or keep climbing?
  2. How do we get past it? Run faster, lower the peak, or hold the storm back? Does clearing the near peak raise a far one?
  3. Secrecy for security. Should a firm race to the capability, hold it, and share it only with trusted defenders, knowing sharing risks leaks?
  4. Secrecy and money. What does a held capability earn, what leaks with each use, and how does that shape holding and racing?
  5. The biggest peak. Is ASI itself the worst stretch, and do the other answers depend on it? The paper answers this only conditionally. Its test assumes the far risk rises with speed.6
Wait, isn’t this just the old argument that AI helps hackers?

Not quite. The paper takes “AI helps attackers first” as a starting assumption and asks a different question: given a dangerous stretch, does speeding through it help? The answer turns on a second clock, the time it takes to deploy fixes, that speed arguments usually leave out.

The paper builds four separate models and never adds them up

The paper builds four small, separate models, each with its own goal and yardstick:7

  • Speed and adoption counts a social risk tally: expected harmful events.
  • Holding the storm counts exposure time saved for defenders.
  • The race counts each racer’s expected payoff.
  • The holder’s choices counts one firm’s private value, in money terms.

The paper never adds these up. Exposure time saved is not harm avoided, and neither is the same as overall welfare. No ranking of policies falls out of the paper.

The paper also extends the first model with health benefits that arrive with the same AI (Part 6). That extension counts health gains and cyber harm in one unit, healthy years of life, for that extension only. It too is never added to the other models.8

“In the model” in this guide means one of these four, on its own terms. Later figures carry a small badge, Model 1 to Model 4, so you can tell which one a picture belongs to (Figure 3).

Who is “the holder”?

A firm that has built a frontier AI capability and is keeping it from general release. It might use it privately, share it with trusted partners, or sell products made with it. The paper’s real case is a lab that restricted its frontier cyber-capable model in 2026.

For policy: read every result below as a statement about one model, under stated conditions. None of them, alone or together, says “the best policy is…”.

Part 2The case for speed needs four things at once

The paper first states the case for speed as strongly as it can. The statement is its own reconstruction. The parts have named supporters, but no single source it read makes the whole case or talks of sprinting through a window.9

Supporters exist. Bruce Schneier wrote that AI has “the potential to upend the longstanding advantage that attack has over defense on the Internet.”10 The lab that restricted its model in 2026 wrote: “In the long run, we expect that defense capabilities will dominate,” though “the transitional period will be fraught.”11 The paper calls that an expectation, not evidence.

The case comes in three versions.

Speed. Planned computing power will push AI until logic, coding and math are practically solved. That helps attackers first, then defenders, through formal verification: machine proof that code does what it should. So the danger is a bounded window. Run through it; caps on computing only keep us exposed longer.

Secret racing. A firm races to the capability, holds it, and shares it with trusted defenders, who fix flaws before attackers catch up. The 2026 lab called its model “a general-purpose, unreleased frontier model” and described an “urgent attempt to put these capabilities to work for defensive purposes.” It expected the window to be short: “Given the rate of AI progress, it will not be long before such capabilities proliferate.”12

Commerce. A holder can build products rivals can’t yet make, while its released models are copied and its sales lead wears away. That pressure pushes it to release. So two races run at once: a commercial race toward general ASI, and a security race for narrow skill in logic, code and math.

What the case needs, all at once (Figure 4):

  • the near peak exists and has a far side;
  • running shortens the time under it more than it raises the danger;
  • a held capability lets defenders cross first;
  • commercial pressure reinforces the security motive or leaves it alone.

The secret and commercial versions also need the narrow skill to be separable from general AI. The paper’s examples all use general models, so they don’t show this.13

One version the paper does not cover: speeding up defense faster than offense, so defenders adopt AI tools before attackers do. Its models put both sides on one clock. It names what to watch (Part 14).14

What is formal verification, and why doesn’t it settle this?

It is a mathematical proof that a program meets its specification. A proof covers only the system it was done for, under its assumptions about hardware, configuration and unproved parts. Studies found bugs where verified and unverified parts meet.15 And a proof protects only the machines that install the proven software (Figure 19).

Part 3What the paper adds is a second clock

The paper’s first result restates, for AI, one by economists Philip Trammell and Leopold Aschenbrenner. If risk comes from development itself, whether speed helps depends on how sharply danger rises with speed. The paper claims nothing new there.16 What it adds is a second clock: the risky period ends only when operators have deployed fixes, on calendar time.

It draws three findings from security economics. Bad incentives cause failures as often as bad design. Patches can be turned into exploits automatically. Fixes reach machines slowly.17 From race models: more knowledge of rivals can make racing more dangerous, though the sign is not settled.18

What may be new is a combination of three things. Two copying clocks push release in opposite directions. A hold slows attackers’ search for flaws nobody has found. And leaks depend on how a capability is used. In the paper’s searches across twenty bodies of work, each work found treats at most one of these. The paper calls that a statement about its searches, not the literature.19

Part 4Does speed help once fixing is the bottleneck?

Side by side, the slower clock sets the length

How long does the dangerous period last? In the fully parallel version, it lasts as long as the slower clock.

  • The capability clock. AI has some distance to travel. Go faster and you cover it sooner.
  • The adoption clock. Operators need time to deploy fixes: patching, retiring old systems, rewriting code. It runs on the calendar.

In the simplest version the two run side by side. At low speeds AI’s progress is the bottleneck; at high speeds, adoption is. The speed threshold is where they take equally long (Figure 5).20

What does “adoption binds” mean?

The adoption clock is the bottleneck. AI has done its part, and the transition still isn’t over because operators are still deploying fixes. Past this point, making AI faster doesn’t shorten the dangerous period at all.

In the model, risk is a tally. It is the danger rate during the transition, times how long it lasts, plus a one-time cost for each step up in capability. If harmful events arrive at random, the tally is their expected number.21 Height alone is not risk: a tall, narrow spike can hold less danger than a lower, wider one (Figure 6).

One premise is built in: the danger rate rises with AI’s speed.22 That is “running thickens the rain.” The evidence sections treat it as a hypothesis.

Why would faster AI make the danger heavier?

In the paper it’s an assumption. The reasoning: attackers’ capability advances with AI’s speed, while defense moves on calendar time, so faster AI widens the gap. The paper calls this a judgment. It also tests the alternative, where danger depends on how capable AI has become rather than how fast it got there.

Past a point, going faster only adds risk

Before the threshold, speed can help; past it, speed only hurts. This is the paper’s base result, which this guide calls the fixed-curb rule:23

  • Before the threshold (AI’s progress is the bottleneck), speeding up shortens the exposure but makes it more intense. It lowers risk only if danger rises less than proportionally with speed. Go faster by some share, and danger rises by a smaller share.
  • Past the threshold (adoption is the bottleneck), speeding up only raises risk. The rain gets heavier and the curb gets no closer.

The past-the-threshold result holds under three conditions:

  • adoption time stays fixed whatever AI’s speed;
  • adoption runs at least partly alongside AI’s progress, not only after it;
  • the step cost doesn’t depend on speed.

Under those, it holds whether danger is set by speed or by how capable AI has become. In the second case it also needs danger at the end of the road to be higher than its average along the way.24 So the best speed, in this model, is at or below the threshold (Figure 7).

Other set-ups change the answer mostly before the threshold

The paper doesn’t know which set-up is real, so it checks several. Fixes might run alongside AI’s progress, wait for the capability, or a mix. Danger might be set by AI’s speed or by the level reached. Figure 8 lays out the cases; in words:25

  • Alongside or mixed, past the threshold: speed raises risk under speed-set danger, and under level-set danger when end-of-road danger exceeds the average.
  • Alongside, before it: under speed-set danger, speed helps only if danger rises less than proportionally. Under level-set danger, speed always helps.
  • Mixed, before it: under speed-set danger, speed helps only if danger rises with speed by less than the share of the transition AI’s progress sets. Under level-set danger, it always helps.
  • Fixes wait for the capability: there is no threshold. Level-set danger: speed always helps. Speed-set danger: it helps only if danger rises slowly enough.

Which kind of danger applies is open. The paper’s judgment favors speed-set danger. It stands in for the gap between attackers, who advance with speed, and defenders on calendar time.

Where is the speed threshold today?

Nobody knows. Locating it needs the capability distance, how far AI must travel to end the transition, and that is unmeasured. The paper’s judgment that adoption binds for some populations (Part 8) is provisional. It does not say where today’s AI speed sits.

Each step up exposes hidden flaws once, by assumption at any speed

Each step up in capability exposes a stock of hidden flaws once. That this cost doesn’t depend on speed is an assumption, not a result.26

Three things set the cost: flaws exposed per step, the chance each is exploited, and damage per exploit. Cutting any one lowers the cost, as long as the other two are above zero. Hardware-bound login could lower the chance; network segmentation or rate limits, the damage. These are proposed channels that need evidence. If exploit success depends on the tools attackers hold when a step arrives, the step cost can depend on speed.

Speed helps past the threshold only if it speeds fixes more than danger

A defender of speed has one way out. Perhaps faster AI also speeds adoption, through automated rollout, verified translation of old code, or automated rewrites. The paper gives exact terms:27

Where adoption is the bottleneck, speeding up lowers risk only if adoption time falls with speed faster, in proportion, than danger rises.

If the step cost also rises with speed, the escape needs more. And the escape is not the threshold moving outward. If adoption binds at today’s speeds, the threshold can move inward while the escape still works.

So where adoption binds, the speed question comes down to one comparison: how fast adoption responds to AI’s speed, against how fast danger does. It is measurable in principle. The paper found no measurement.

What would change the speed answer?

Evidence that AI shortens adoption itself, faster than it raises danger. Signs to watch: verified translation and rewriting reaching production code, and AI patching that narrows the gap between flaws found and fixed. Another sign: retirement and migration speeding up across sectors, not only in the fastest channels.

Each group of operators has its own threshold

Big firms, small operators, open-source maintainers and industrial-control operators each have their own adoption time. So the threshold is a spread, not a point. With several populations, speed lowers overall risk only if the gain where AI’s progress is the bottleneck outweighs the loss where adoption is (Figure 10).28

For policy: the case for speed depends on a threshold nobody has located, and on how adoption responds to AI, which nobody has measured. Past the threshold, under the stated conditions, speed alone does not help.

Part 5A peak can exist, but the peak model has no AI in it

Danger peaks only if flaws pour in faster than they drain, then slower

Imagine a bathtub. New code pours flaws in through the tap; patching and retirement drain them. The water level is the stock of exploitable flaws, and danger rises and falls with it. (An illustration of the paper’s flaw-stock model.)

The paper’s peak result, here called the flaw tide:29

  • Danger rises and later falls only if at some point flaws pour in faster than they drain, and later more slowly.
  • Protection of new code matters. Suppose protection, such as memory-safe languages, spreads through new code, cutting the inflow toward a residue it can’t reach. Take the example where the tub starts with no exploitable flaws and removal is constant. Then a peak is guaranteed when protection spreads more slowly than removal works. When protection spreads faster, a peak still comes unless enough new code stays unprotected. The peak is pre-ASI only if it arrives before ASI.
  • A peak can be followed by a plateau. They are not alternatives. The plateau’s height depends only on the unprotected residue and the drain rate, not on how fast protection spread.

Two policy readings follow, in the same example of an empty start and constant removal. If protection eventually covers all new code, there is always a peak. It is pre-ASI only if it arrives before ASI. If a residue remains, faster protection can remove the peak, leaving a steady rise to the same plateau.

So long-run danger depends on what protection can’t reach and how fast flaws are removed. And if today’s stock is already high enough that removal outpaces new arrivals, no peak lies ahead in this model.

Two limits. Exploitation per flaw is held constant. If attackers’ skill per flaw rises faster than the stock falls, danger can climb while the tub drains. Most important: the peak model contains no AI speed or capability. A peak can exist in the flaw-stock dynamics. Whether AI creates or moves it is not modelled.30

So does AI create a peak or not?

The paper doesn’t say, because its peak model doesn’t include AI. It shows when a stock of flaws would rise then fall, and that a peak and a plateau can both happen. Tying that path to AI’s speed is listed as future work.

Trading a near storm for a far one needs a price for the far one

Suppose a near cyber peak and a far one at ASI. Speed might lower the first and raise the second. The paper’s two-storms test:31

  • Speed lowers total loss only if it lowers the near loss and the near stakes, compared with the far ones, are big enough to outweigh the far loss’s rise. Past the threshold, the near loss rises too, so no ratio of stakes justifies speeding up. That holds for this two-loss test; Part 6 adds a benefit.
  • Valuing the future less makes speed less attractive: the bar for speed to help gets stricter.
  • If the far loss comes sooner when AI is faster, speed brings it nearer.
  • If both are catastrophes in sequence, speed lowers the total chance only if the drop in near risk, weighted by surviving the far one, beats the rise in far risk, weighted by surviving the near one. Here the far catastrophe’s chance means its chance once the near one has been survived.

The test judges small changes. It also counts nothing speed buys beyond the two losses; Part 6 adds one benefit, health gains from the same AI. A large jump can cut total loss where the small-change test fails, so large moves must be judged in levels.

It also assumes the far risk rises with speed. In this two-loss test, where adoption binds and responds to speed more slowly than danger does, both peaks point the same way and their ranking doesn’t matter. Where speed lowers the near peak, the comparison needs a value for the far loss. The paper doesn’t supply one (Figure 12). With a health benefit counted too, the far loss can matter even where near harm rises (Part 6).

For policy: long-run danger rests on unreachable code and removal speed. In the two-loss test, whether superintelligence is the bigger peak matters only where speed lowers the near one.

Part 6Life-saving benefits give speed a second reason, with conditions

So far, speed buys one thing in Model 1: a shorter stay in the rain. But the AI that arms attackers may also do good that has nothing to do with cyber defense. Does a benefit that saves lives change the speed answer?

It can. In the model, a health benefit gives a second reason to run, even past Part 4’s speed threshold, which this part calls the cyber threshold. This holds only while the benefit still waits on AI’s progress, not on its own trials and approval. And it holds only if the benefit brought forward outweighs the added cyber harm.32

Running to a pharmacy helps only if the counter serves you

Here is an analogy, not a claim from the paper. You cross the street in the rain to a pharmacy. If the counter serves you on arrival, running gets you the medicine sooner. That can be worth some heavier rain. If your order is still being filled, arriving sooner gets you nothing sooner. Then only the rain question is left (Figure 13).

The paper’s motivating example is protein structure. AlphaFold was, in its developers’ words, “the first computational method that can regularly predict protein structures with atomic accuracy even in cases in which no similar structure is known.” Half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper “for protein structure prediction.” Neither source says AlphaFold shortens drug development. That such capabilities bring health benefits sooner is this part’s premise, not a finding.33

One yardstick for this extension: healthy years of life

The paper adds a benefit to Model 1 and keeps everything else. One AI speed moves both roads: the road to the end of the cyber transition, and the road to the capability the benefit needs. The two distances may differ.

The model holds both adoption times fixed as AI speed changes. It also holds fixed the share of each adoption that waits for the capability. The cyber and benefit timings need not match. Benefits count only up to one fixed calendar date; this guide calls that span the period counted. The date is late enough to include the whole cyber transition and its step losses.

To weigh lives against cyber harm, the extension needs one unit. It uses DALYs, disability-adjusted life years. One DALY “can be thought of as one lost year of ‘healthy’ life.” Counting DALYs averted puts deaths and illness in one unit. Cyber harm enters in the same unit, at a fixed number of DALYs per harmful event.34

The result is a balance: health benefit minus cyber loss. It is a conditional balance, not total welfare. It assumes a steady benefit that lasts once it arrives, and it counts each cyber harm once. The yardstick holds for this extension only. The other three models keep theirs, and nothing is added across them.

Past the threshold, the benefit must outweigh the extra cyber loss

Past the cyber threshold, speeding up can still raise the balance. It does so only if the benefit brought forward outweighs the extra cyber loss (Figure 14).35

  • The benefit brought forward is the benefit per year times the part of its wait that AI’s progress sets. Speed up by one percent, and that part shrinks by one percent.
  • The extra cyber loss is how sharply danger rises with speed, times the cyber harm while fixes deploy.

This exact test has a narrow scope. Danger is set by AI’s speed. Speed is past the cyber threshold. The benefit still waits on AI and arrives within the period counted. And the future is not discounted. Other cases have their own tests in the paper. Nobody has measured either side.

Once the benefit waits on its own clock, the old test decides

The second reason to run can vanish. It counts only while AI’s progress sets when the benefit arrives. Once the benefit waits only on trials, approval and uptake, faster AI brings it no sooner. That happens past the benefit’s own threshold; under sequential timing there is none. Running then gets no one to the far curb sooner. Whether it keeps you drier is the fixed-curb rule’s question again (Figure 15).36

What that means depends on the cyber clock:

  • Below the cyber threshold, the fixed-curb rule decides, as in Figure 8. Under speed-set danger, speed helps only if danger rises slowly enough. Under level-set danger, it helps if average danger along the way is above zero; otherwise it makes no difference.
  • Past the cyber threshold, speed only adds cyber harm when danger is set by speed. When danger is set by the level reached, it adds harm if end-of-road danger is above its average.

While the benefit still waits on AI, it also widens the range where speed helps below the cyber threshold. Speed can then help even when danger rises faster than speed. In one illustrative case, danger rises twice as fast as speed, in proportion, and the cyber loss rises with speed. Yet the benefit grows 16 times as fast as the cyber loss, so the balance still improves.

The benefit has its own clock: trials, approval, manufacture, uptake

A benefit like a new drug reaches patients only after trials, approval, manufacture and uptake. These run on their own clock. A report for the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services states that “the clinical phase lasts an average of around 95 months compared to 31 months for the non-clinical phase.” It attributes that figure to an earlier study.

The report’s non-clinical stage includes early research such as target discovery. Across therapeutic areas, Phase 3 is the longest stage, at 38.0 months. FDA review averages 16.2 months, a figure that includes the sponsor’s own time. The clinical phases “may overlap.”37 That overlap does not show how a benefit’s adoption overlaps AI’s progress.

One AI-discovered drug programme reports about 18 months from target discovery to a preclinical candidate. It reports under 30 months from the start of target discovery to the end of phase 0/1 testing. That is one programme, reported by its developers, and its phase 2a trial then followed on the clinical clock. The paper found no peer-reviewed study of AI’s effect on total development time across drugs. That is a statement about its searches.38

The paper’s reading, not the report’s claim, is that much of the wait for a new drug lies in stages AI’s progress does not set.

The model gives the benefit the same three timings as the cyber side (Figure 16):

  • Parallel: the benefit’s adoption runs alongside AI’s progress, and the slower one sets arrival.
  • Mixed: a share of the adoption, which the paper calls mB, waits for the capability; the rest runs alongside.
  • Sequential: adoption starts only once the capability arrives.

Faster AI shortens only AI’s part of the wait. And the share of adoption that waits is not the share of the wait that AI sets. In one illustrative check, with mB at 0.6, AI’s progress sets a share of 0.81 of the whole wait.

The cyber threshold and the benefit’s own threshold may come in either order, or coincide. A clock with sequential timing has no threshold. If the benefit’s own adoption takes as long as the period counted, or longer, no benefit counts at any speed. Otherwise the benefit starts to count only once speed brings its arrival inside that period. Where speed crosses one of these points, compare the change on each side.

Does each step of speed gain less benefit as speed rises?

Each step of speed brings the benefit forward by a share of the wait that AI still sets. When that wait is long, a step gains a lot. When the benefit is close, a step gains little. So, without discounting, each equal percentage rise in speed brings the benefit forward less as speed rises. This holds while AI sets the benefit’s arrival and the benefit arrives within the period counted.39

A best speed past the threshold is possible, not guaranteed

The paper works one example with illustrative values, not estimates (Figure 17). The cyber threshold sits at speed 1 and the benefit’s own threshold at speed 4. The balance peaks at 6, at speed 2, twice the cyber threshold. It is 1, 5, 5 and 3 at speeds 0.5, 1, 4 and 6. At the best speed the cyber loss is rising; the benefit pays for it.40

The same example shows that benefits do not always justify speed. At speed 5 the benefit is large, but it no longer moves with speed. Going from 5 to 6 lowers the balance from 4 to 3. A large benefit is not, by itself, a reason to go faster.

In general, a best speed exists when the inputs have no jumps and speed is limited to a fixed range above zero. It can sit at either end of that range, at a threshold, or at the speed where the benefit starts to count. It can also sit where the balance is flat, perhaps along a level stretch. A flat point alone does not prove the best speed; compare every candidate. Without both limits on the range, a best speed need not exist.

Discounting cuts both sides, with no general direction

Suppose future health and future cyber losses are both discounted from the same date. A late benefit then counts for less. But the cyber loss changes too, so the effect on the overall sign has no general direction. WHO’s current burden estimates use “No discounting for time,” so any discount rate here is the paper’s modelling choice.41

Discounting also changes the step costs. A step loss of fixed size counts more when faster AI brings it earlier. That weakens the case for speed. Level-set danger needs its own discounted calculation, and the undiscounted answers can reverse. In one illustrative check, a gain from speed became a small loss.

The model assumes one AI speed moves both; narrow tools may not

The extension assumes full coupling: the AI that arms attackers is the AI that brings the benefit. That is an assumption, not a finding. Narrow tools, such as specialized structure-prediction models, could advance a benefit on a clock of their own. Then speeding general AI would not bring that benefit forward. The paper names this limit and does not model it; no sign is known (Figure 18).42

Biological misuse and hospital attacks are named, not modelled

The capability that speeds drug discovery may also aid biological attack. That risk lies outside this balance, and the paper does not model it.

Cyber harm also reaches health directly. One study found that ransomware attacks on hospitals “decrease hospital volume by 17–24 percent during the initial attack week.” It also found: “Among patients already admitted to the hospital when a ransomware attack begins, in-hospital mortality increases by 34–38 percent.” Another found that targeted hospital attacks “may be associated with disruptions of health care delivery at nontargeted hospitals within a community.” None of these studies estimates DALYs per attack, so no source gives the health loss per harmful event.43

These findings point to a link the model leaves out. Attacks on health systems may cut the benefit itself, which the extension holds constant.

With a benefit, the two-storms test gains a term

Part 5’s two-storms test found that, past the threshold, no ratio of stakes justifies speeding up. That holds for its two losses. With a benefit in the same units, speed can improve the balance even while near harm rises. Then the far storm can matter there too. If far risk rises with speed, speed helps only if the benefit gained, less the near loss’s rise, beats the far loss’s rise. The paper adds no new result about the far loss.44

For policy: faster AI and shorter trials are different levers

If the goal includes health benefits from the same capability, two levers differ.45

  • Faster AI brings the benefit forward only while AI’s progress sets its arrival. With speed-set danger, it also raises cyber danger.
  • A shorter benefit clock, through trial and approval capacity, manufacturing and uptake, can bring the benefit forward without raising cyber danger. That such capacity shortens the benefit’s clock without changing cyber danger is an assumption, not a result.

The two levers work differently. A shorter benefit clock raises the benefit’s total only weakly: strictly only when it brings arrival forward within the period counted. Under parallel timing, while AI sets arrival, it changes nothing. While AI sets arrival and that stays so, a shorter benefit clock leaves the undiscounted return to extra speed unchanged. With discounting, it raises that return when some adoption waits for the capability. If a shorter benefit clock changes which clock sets arrival, that change needs its own check.

One study found that, after data breaches, deaths within 30 days of a heart attack rose by as much as 0.36 percentage points within three years. Its authors associate that rise with remediation, which they did not directly observe.46 Which lever should come first needs numbers the paper does not have.

What to measure: three parts of a benefit’s wait, separately

For AI-assisted drug programmes, three quantities should be measured separately. They are the wait for the capability, the benefit’s own adoption time, and the share of that adoption that waits for the capability. No source the paper read gives any of the three, or the DALYs lost per harmful cyber event (Part 14).47

For policy: in the model, a life-saving benefit is a real second reason to run. It counts only while the benefit still waits on AI, and only if it outweighs the added cyber harm. Once its own trials and approval set its pace, faster AI buys no earlier benefit.

Part 7AI is advancing fast on tasks that are cheap to check

The paper’s capability snapshot, from late 2026, shows fast progress on tasks a computer can check automatically:48

  • FrontierMath: Epoch’s own runs put the best scores at 93.7% on Tiers 1–3 and 100% on Tier 4, whose private set has 41 problems. OpenAI models hold both; FrontierMath was developed with OpenAI funding, and OpenAI has exclusive access to a subset.
  • ARC-AGI-2’s semi-private set: best score 95%.
  • A perfect score at the 2026 International Mathematical Olympiad, on a lab’s own account of official marking.
  • Five PutnamBench entries report all 672 problems solved; the saved records don’t document public verification.

Coding is not saturated: FrontierSWE and FrontierCode best scores were 65.5% and 54.6%. METR’s agent-task measure warns, “Measurements above 16 hrs are unreliable with our current task suite,” and hasn’t been updated since 8 September 2026. The paper gives no doubling estimate.

Isn’t 100% on a math benchmark proof that math is solved?

No. The paper says the record shows rapid progress on selected tasks that are cheap to check, not that logic, coding and math are practically solved. In its untested judgment, for research math and real software, deciding what to check may be the bottleneck.

Three cautions apply:

  • Scaling laws are fitted to a training measure called loss, not to skills. Across a tenfold rise in training compute, average forecasting errors for skills were 6 percentage points for a task group and 18 for single tasks.49
  • Rice’s theorem rules out one method that decides every interesting property of every program. It leaves room for special methods; the paper calls it a mathematical limit, not a ceiling on useful verification.50
  • Real proofs have edges. seL4’s proofs assume correct hardware and boot code. Testing found six wrong-code bugs in an early CompCert compiler’s unverified front end. Three verified distributed systems had sixteen bugs, mostly where verified and unverified parts meet.51

And the security examples all use general models. There is no evidence yet that a narrow code-only system could supply the capability alone.

Part 8AI can find flaws fast; fixes still run on the calendar

Attackers may gain first, but that is a hypothesis

The paper treats “AI favors attackers first” as a hypothesis. These are reported lab and competition results, not a matched comparison with human experts. The lab figures are the lab’s own, without independent replication:52

  • DARPA’s AI Cyber Challenge (August 2025 final): automated systems found 54 of 63 synthetic vulnerabilities and patched 43. They found 18 real ones and supplied 11 patches.
  • One lab, February 2026: more than 500 high-severity open-source vulnerabilities found and validated, by its count.
  • That lab, April 2026: its restricted model built working browser-engine exploits 181 times, against two successes in several hundred attempts for its earlier model. The test set-up left out the browser’s sandbox. Since 181 exceeds the 112 unique reports from that work, the count includes repeats.
  • That lab, June 2026: from 18 Firefox security patches public for at least 90 days, it built eight exploits. The first took under an hour of test time, the eighth roughly twelve hours. The plots use the fastest of three trials per stage, in a standalone JavaScript-shell test rather than the full browser. Test runtimes, not field times.
  • The UK AI Security Institute: it tracks the length of cyber task frontier models could finish with 80 percent reliability, in its narrow suite. By its February 2026 estimate, that length had doubled every 4.7 months since late 2024. The fit “is not a future prediction, nor a fixed law.”

In the field, Mandiant studied 138 vulnerabilities disclosed in 2023 and exploited. It reported an average time-to-exploit of five days excluding 15 outliers, 47 days including them.53

What is a zero-day?

A flaw attackers exploit before the maker has a fix, so defenders have had “zero days.” An n-day is already public, often with a patch available, but still unpatched on many machines.

Capability is not harm. These results show capability, not a measured attack–defense balance, and not harm.

Google’s threat-intelligence group tracked 90 zero-days exploited in 2025, against 78 in 2024, as its 2025 review counts them. Counting n-days, it tracked 127 exploited vulnerabilities in 2025 and 141 from January to August 2026. CISA added 186 entries to its known-exploited catalogue in 2024 and 245 in 2025. None isolates AI. The Google group calls exploitation of AI-discovered flaws “an early indicator rather than an established trend.”54 So the paper holds, with high confidence, that the capability exists, and with low confidence what it does to net harm.

What is the KEV list?

CISA’s public catalogue of Known Exploited Vulnerabilities: flaws the U.S. cyber-defense agency knows attackers have used. Each entry has a date added and often a weakness-type code (a CWE tag). It reflects CISA’s listing choices as well as attackers.

The disclosure dashboard’s counts don’t measure what share of each batch got patched, how fast fixes deploy, or how many systems stay exposed. The lab calls its patch count a lagging indicator. It says it discloses only part of what it finds because “independent human triage and review is the rate limiting step.”55

Which kinds of fixing can make adoption the bottleneck?

The paper splits adoption in two.56 Patching flaws AI finds plausibly waits for the capability, so it lengthens the transition without deciding whether adoption is the bottleneck. Retirement and migration plausibly run alongside AI’s progress; only they can make adoption bind.

Patching is slow:

  • At 12 firms, across 190 million vulnerabilities, median remediation was 90 days, with 32.5% open after 180 days (data to late 2018).
  • On 8.4 million client computers, the median time to patch half of vulnerable hosts was 45 days. The study reports that “only 28% of the patches in our study reach 95% of the vulnerable hosts during our observation period.”
  • In open source, over half of flaws were patched more than a week before disclosure, so attackers watching repositories “can often get a jump of weeks to months.”

Retirement and migration are slower:

  • Agencies reported three of ten critical federal legacy systems modernized by February 2025.
  • HTTPS in Chrome went from about 30–45% of navigations in 2015 to about 95–99% around 2020, then largely plateaued.
  • Chrome planned about two and a half years from announcing the SHA-1 phase-out to removing support.
  • IPv6 took about 27 years to pass half of access to Google on one day.
Why does patching count differently from retiring old systems?

Timing. A patch for a flaw AI finds can’t exist before the flaw is found, so patching follows the capability. Retiring a system or switching protocols can happen whenever operators choose. So only that work, running alongside AI’s progress, can set the speed threshold. Patching can still prolong the danger after AI has done its part; it just doesn’t set the threshold.

The paper’s judgment, and its limits. From the retirement and migration series, the paper judges that adoption already binds for the populations they cover: critical federal legacy systems and web-protocol migration. That is a provisional judgment, not an established result, and not a judgment about patching in general. The lags show deployment is slow. They don’t locate today’s speed against the threshold, and don’t show adoption speeding up enough for the escape.

One proposed reason, in Ross Anderson and Tyler Moore’s words: “Systems are particularly prone to failure when the person guarding them is not the person who suffers when they fail.”57

What do CISA’s listings show about flaw types?

The flaw tide’s plateau depends on code protection can’t reach. The paper made its own calculation from CISA’s catalogue, using entries with a usable weakness tag. It compared entries added November 2021 through 2023 (920) with those added January 2024 to 20 October 2026 (659). The access-policy share rose from 19% (171 of 920) to 27% (180 of 659). The memory-corruption share fell from 33% (299 of 920) to 19% (125 of 659). Two robustness checks leave the direction unchanged. The mix reflects CISA’s listing practice as well as attackers.58

What is memory corruption, and why track its share?

Bugs where a program reads or writes memory it shouldn’t, common in C and C++. Memory-safe languages prevent that class in new code, so its share is one rough sign of protection spreading. Access-policy flaws, mistakes about who may do what, are untouched by memory safety.

Some attacks skip software, and many defenses need no frontier AI

Some attacks skip software. Scattered Spider phones IT help desks “to reset passwords and/or transfer MFA tokens.” FinCEN reports criminals have reportedly used generative AI to impersonate “an executive or other trusted employee.” Adaptive attacks bypassed 12 recent defenses against AI prompt attacks, “with attack success rate above 90% for most.” A design-level defense, CaMeL, solved 77% of a benchmark’s tasks “with provable security,” against 84% undefended.59

Lowering the peak doesn’t require running faster. Differential technology development holds that it “may be beneficial to delay risk-increasing technologies and preferentially advance risk-reducing defensive, safety, or substitute technologies.”60 In the paper’s judgment, many defenses need no frontier AI: patch distribution, legacy retirement, memory-safe languages for new code, post-quantum migration, hardware-bound login, and verified kernels and libraries. AI bug-finding, patching and proof-writing serve attackers too, for general models.

The deciding comparison has never been measured

The crux is open, and empirical. Where adoption binds, the case for speed is right only if frontier AI shortens adoption faster, in proportion, than it raises danger.

The evidence on AI translating old code comes from benchmarks. On 100 C repositories, the best model tested solved 15 tasks in one attempt. Another system passed a formal check on 42% of 1,394 competition programs.61 DARPA’s automated patches are patch creation, not deployment. The paper has no measurement of how adoption responds to speed.

For policy: these defenses need no frontier AI, in the paper’s judgment. Whether speed helps depends on a number nobody has measured.

Part 9Holding a capability back helps defenders only for a while

The head start is worth at most its length

An umbrella lent for part of a long walk keeps you dry for that part only. A secret capability works the same way. It splits the clocks for a while: some attackers have access anyway, such as states with their own frontier AI. The rest arrive when the window closes: open-weights catch-up, a rival’s release, a leak or theft.

The paper’s head-start limit:62

A covered defender saves, in exposure time, at most the shorter of two spans: how long the window lasts, and how long that defender needs to deploy fixes. And only against attackers who lacked access.

For defenders who deploy well within the window, the share of exposure saved approaches the share of attackers locked out. For those needing many windows’ worth of time, the share saved shrinks. It falls to roughly the window’s average length divided by their deployment time, times that locked-out share. This assumes a window with a steady chance of ending (Figure 23).

Two parts carry policy weight:

  • The overall cap. Across all defenders, a window can remove only a capped share of total exposure. The cap is the exposure held by fast defenders plus the program’s coverage of everyone else, all scaled by the locked-out share. That holds whatever the window’s length.
  • The exception. Suppose harm grows faster than exposure time, so the longest exposures cost most. Then “worth at most its length” fails in harm units: the head start removes the costliest slice. The paper gives a replacement bound.

Conditions: harm grows in step with exposure, coverage doesn’t depend on the window, and attackers resume at their old pace, with no backlog. Fast patchers gain most in proportion; whether they gain most in total needs evidence. The holder’s partners are likely large firms that already patch fast, a judgment.

What are “open weights”?

A model’s weights are the numbers that make it work. An open-weight model publishes them: anyone can run and modify it, strip its safeguards, and it can’t be recalled. A closed-weight model keeps them private and serves the capability through an interface.

How long a window lasts is unmeasured.63 The lab restricted its model on 7 April 2026. An open-weight model, GLM-5.3, was released on 14 August; its weights went public two weeks later. The restricting lab reported results on one exploit benchmark. The open model built end-to-end exploits in 50 of 410 attempts, against 56 of 410 for the restricted model.

NIST’s AI standards center placed it about four months behind the U.S. frontier among released models it evaluated. Epoch earlier gave a 90% interval of five to twenty-two months for open models’ benchmark lag. But catching up on benchmarks shows neither how fast copies spread nor how long a security window lasts. The paper calls window length unverified.

Fixes are roadmaps, and holds protect unfound flaws

To protect outsiders from flaws it finds, the holder must ship fixes. But a public patch can guide attackers.

Why would a published patch help attackers?

A patch shows exactly what changed, pointing to where the flaw was. Researchers showed in 2008 that exploits can be generated automatically from patches. Every unpatched machine is then a known target.

The paper’s “fixes are roadmaps” result compares holding the capability with releasing it, flaw by flaw, in three groups:64

  1. Flaws the holder finds and patches. Suppose the patch comes out on the same date either way. Then holding the whole capability adds nothing over an embargo on that flaw. That holds unless attackers would have found it themselves first; then holding can lower exposure. And if attackers with the capability turn patches into exploits faster, a hold protects these flaws too, for at most the window.
  2. Flaws the holder never finds. Holding delays attackers’ own search for them, for at most the window: the head-start limit again.
  3. Deployment-only tools. Fix patch dates and the start of attacks. If a tool only helps defenders install published patches faster, withholding it never lowers outsiders’ exposure. It raises exposure whenever the tool would have shortened deployment while attacks were under way.

The paper’s model illustration of the second group uses 100 flaws, 90 never found by the holder. Expected exposure is 435 units under release and about 413.6 under a hold, though no patch ships for the 90. These are inputs chosen to test a claim, not measurements.

Three readings follow:

  • A hold of the whole capability does protect outsiders, through unfound flaws, for at most the window. So the right comparison for a hold is release with safeguards that keep attackers out, not a flaw-by-flaw embargo. Whether such safeguards are feasible is open.
  • The deployment-only result needs its conditions; if a tool also produces patches, it needs its own analysis.
  • Coordinated dates can fail. Spectre was reported to vendors on 1 June 2017, for a coordinated date of 9 January 2018. It was published six days early “because of existing public reports and growing speculation.”65

Sharing with no one, or with everyone, can be best

Each partner adds coverage and leak risk together. The paper’s coalition result: either extreme can be best, sharing with no one or with everyone eligible. Something in between is best if the first partner adds benefit and the last subtracts it.66

Admitting a rival raises exposure saved only if it lowers the overall chance the window ends. The rival-release risk removed must exceed the leak risk added. That needs coverage, deployment times and attackers’ access fixed, and a rival that can commit not to release. If admission also covers the rival’s own systems, savings can rise even as the window shortens. In 2024 RAND judged protecting internet-connected models against the most capable attackers “currently not feasible.”67

A secret window raises racing when it widens the winner–loser payoff gap

A secret window is also a prize. Imagine a raffle where tickets cost money. Your chance of winning is your share of all tickets, and every ticket adds to a risk all players share. (This is the paper’s contest model in everyday terms.)

The paper’s winner’s-prize result:68

  • Racing effort depends on the gap between what the winner and the loser get.
  • A window only the winner gets raises racing in proportion to how much it widens that gap, and the shared risk with it.
  • A window both racers get leaves racing unchanged.

Whether extra racing harms the world is a separate question, and not automatic. It depends on the window’s real value to everyone else, the holder’s own real gain, and the cost of extra racing net of anything useful it produces. A private value bigger than the world’s gain doesn’t by itself make secrecy harmful. The claim that “even a modest escalation” by rivals outweighs the cyber gain is an empirical premise, not a theorem.

How rivals answer a suspected secret lead has no settled sign. One lab wrote that every similarly capable model other than GLM-5.3 had been “released with safeguards or through limited access programs.” The paper says that fits norm-following, risk management or commercial choice, and identifies no motive.69

Release with safeguards beats holding only if wider access speeds fixes enough

Toby Shevlane calls this structured capability access: the developer offers “controlled, arm’s length interactions” with a system, and “must both restrict how the AI system can be used, and prevent the user from circumventing these restrictions through modification or reverse engineering” of the system.70

The paper’s safeguarded-release test first holds defenders’ schedules unchanged. Then release with safeguards raises outsiders’ exposure, if at all, by the chance safeguards fail times the extra exposure failure causes. It lowers exposure only if giving every defender access improves their fixing schedules by more than that. The test sets aside new risks serving the model might create, such as theft or copying. Adding them would raise exposure further.71

The test has a narrow frame. It compares two choices only: release now behind safeguards, or hold with no later release at all. It keeps everyone’s future access routes the same under both. And it assumes defenders’ schedules don’t depend on which events happen or on whether the safeguards fail.

What is “release with safeguards”?

Releasing a capability through a controlled interface with limits on use, rather than publishing the model or holding it entirely. If safeguards hold, all defenders get it and attackers stay out. If they fail, attackers get it at once. How well real safeguards resist stripping and copying is unmeasured.

For policy: in the model a hold helps covered defenders by at most the window, only against locked-out attackers. Exposure time saved is not harm avoided or welfare gained. The fair test of a hold is release with safeguards, and it turns on safeguard durability and window length, both unverified.

Part 10A held capability earns in four places, and each leaks differently

What leaks depends on the use, not on secrecy as such

A holder can earn in four places:72

  • the inference market (coding agents, chat, programming interfaces), where it earns nothing more until release and then its lead wears away;
  • defense with partners, security while the window lasts;
  • products in other markets (voice, video, agents, drug and materials discovery, design);
  • internal research, using the model to build the next one, hard to see from outside.

What leaks depends on the use, not on secrecy as such. What follows is the paper’s judgment, which it says needs evidence. Release without effective safeguards leaks most of the capability. Sharing with partners leaks a lot, likely more with each one. An interactive product leaks a lot where it overlaps code and logic. An artifact, like a drug candidate or design, leaks the output, not the engine, plus a signal the engine exists. Internal research leaks little directly.

The paper offers three hypotheses to test. First, artifacts may let a firm profit while holding, so firms with rich artifact markets could hold longer. Second, using an edge may reveal it, and value may attract theft. The paper found no documented case of a capability copied soon after a demonstration, and no measure of theft against value. Third, artifacts may turn a temporary lead into lasting assets: patents, designs, data, locked-in customers.

Two kinds of copying push release in opposite directions

Imagine a bakery with an unlaunched recipe. Rivals copying its current bread shrink sales each month, which pushes it to launch sooner. If rivals will copy the new recipe quickly too, launching is worth less, which pushes it to wait. (An illustration of the two copy clocks.)

The paper’s two-copy-clocks result treats timing as a stopping problem:73

  • Faster copying of the current public model erodes revenue, so holding costs more. Release comes sooner.
  • Faster expected copying of the model it would release lowers what release is worth. For revenue that rises in a straight line with the lead, and for a broad family of curved patterns, release comes later.

These directions are local: they hold at a release date in the middle of the range, other things fixed. Under revenue that flattens out at the top, faster post-release copying can bring release sooner. The second direction also holds the risk of losing the secret fixed. If faster copying also raises that risk, and release is worth more than what survives a loss, it adds a push the other way.

Wait, so faster copying makes firms wait? Isn’t that backwards?

It depends which copying. Copying of what’s on the market pushes release sooner; expected copying of what you’d release pushes it later, for the named revenue shapes. With one copy rate for both, the net sign isn’t settled. In the paper’s scans, faster common copying delayed release in all 56 cases of one grid. In a wider scan it delayed release in 148 of 161 draws, against 13 where it hastened release. The paper calls these frequencies over chosen draws, not a result.

Corners are common: often it’s release now, or hold until the secret is lost. With straight-line revenue, a mid-range release date is strictly best only under one condition. The released model must be copied faster than the current one by more than the risk of losing the secret. That’s necessary, not sufficient. Below that rate, the best choice is a corner: release at once, or hold until the secret is lost. At exactly that rate, the firm can sometimes be indifferent among every date. With constant earnings and no public model to lose, it’s all-or-nothing, and faster post-release copying pushes toward holding. Whether released models are copied that much faster is unmeasured.

So the inference market doesn’t, in general, force held capabilities out on a copy-speed schedule. The pressure is real, from erosion of the current lead. But expected quick copying after release is, for the named revenue shapes and at fixed loss risk, a reason to hold. Which clock wins is empirical; it needs prices per unit of capability and customers’ switching and loyalty.74

The commercial lead never outlasts attackers’ exclusion

The paper’s extension separates two windows.75 The commercial window is how long the holder keeps its market lead. The attacker-access window is how long locked-out attackers stay out.

Five events can end the lead: a rival’s comparable open release; a rival’s closed release whose safeguards fail; one whose safeguards work; theft of the weights; a partner’s leak. All end the commercial lead. All but the working closed release also give attackers lasting capability.

So the commercial window is never longer than the attacker-access window. That rests on a premise: theft and leaks hand rivals a commercial substitute at once. Covert theft that armed only attackers would require revising it.

The paper judges, without evidence yet, that an open copy erodes the lead faster. Its safeguards can be stripped: “abliterated versions” of the 2026 open-weight model appeared “within days.” It can’t be recalled.76

The reach limit. Levers that bind only covered domestic developers can’t stretch the expected attacker-access window past a ceiling. What they don’t reach sets that ceiling: unbound developers, theft and leaks. This assumes those outside access routes stay above zero and unchanged; reducing theft or leaks changes the ceiling. This ranks no interventions.77 The extension sets aside the holder’s profit, so none of it carries over to the paper’s commercial results.

Side uses, theft and lasting assets change what a secret is worth

The side-business rule. A new use of a held capability is worth adding when what it earns, per unit of extra leak risk, exceeds what a leak would destroy. Theft drawn by earnings counts as added risk.78 The rule assumes the hold ends only by involuntary loss. With a live release option it’s the wrong yardstick. A use the firm commits to can pass the rule and still lower the firm’s value, by destroying the release it was waiting for. An optional use couldn’t. The rule takes given numbers; nothing follows about real products.

The theft ceiling. Theft caps a secret’s value only if theft eventually rises at least in proportion to what the secret earns. If theft rises more slowly, tracks total value rather than earnings, or security is cheap enough, there’s no cap. The proportionality premise is the paper’s own; RAND ties needed security to capabilities and stakes, not earnings.79

Perishable leads. A lead others copy is worth less than one they can’t, a valuation identity.80 Faster copying raises a lasting prize’s value relative to a wearing lead, not its own value. It says nothing about how hard firms chase it.

Durable assets: lasting earnings make each step’s race fiercer. Suppose part of each step’s earnings is, from the start, something copying can’t erode.81 Then the decisive, lasting prize looks smaller relative to a step’s. Winning each step is worth more, so step racing rises. That holds only if the winner alone gets those lasting earnings. It also needs the step prize to count none of the value of going on to win the decisive prize.

The paper also works one specification. There the two contests share one cost that rises with combined effort, so total effort can change. Racers bear none of the catastrophe loss themselves. In it, effort toward the decisive prize falls as the step prize grows. Total risk rises only if decisive-race effort isn’t too much riskier per unit (the paper gives the cut-off). With separate budgets, decisive effort doesn’t move. The evidence to gather: labs’ patents and product launches outside the inference market, timed against capability jumps.

Using the model to build the next one is hard to see

Using a held model to build the next serves both money and security and is hard to see. It could speed the general race with little visible sign; the paper calls that a hypothesis and doesn’t model it. It also deepens a signaling problem: outsiders can’t tell holding for security from holding for profit.82

For policy: commercial pressure to release is real but doesn’t set a copy-speed schedule. These results describe one firm’s private choices, not what is best for the world.

Part 11Actors race anyway, and restraint needs detection

If speed doesn’t lower the near peak where adoption binds, why race? The paper’s contest results answer in four parts (Figures 31 and 32):83

  1. A finite prize limits sensible effort. With a fixed prize and steady cost per unit, spending more than the prize is worth is always worse than spending nothing. In the raffle game, the best effort depends on the rival’s, so no single effort is best against everything.
  2. A one-sided cap helps a little. If one racer caps its effort at a positive level below the usual one, the rival’s best response falls too. The capper wins less often, but total effort falls.
  3. Racing exceeds what racers would jointly choose, unless it has enough public value they don’t capture.
  4. Restraint needs detection. In a repeated race where cheating is caught with some chance and triggers racing forever after, restraint holds only if detection reaches a threshold. For impatient racers that threshold can exceed certainty, so no detection technology is enough. Under full secrecy, restraint fails. In a one-shot race, restraint holds only if the expected penalty on detection is at least half the stake.
Can’t labs just agree not to race?

In the model, only with detection good enough to catch cheating often enough and a credible penalty. The model excludes false alarms, which in real monitoring can collapse restraint with nobody cheating. Arms-control research shows why such deals are rare: monitoring must be open enough to assure compliance but not so open it threatens security.

Other pushes sit alongside. U.S. policy frames AI as a race: “The United States is in a race to achieve global dominance in artificial intelligence (AI).” The paper judges that sunk capital and falling computing prices may push labs to ship. Neither has a measured size.84

Part 12The models settle three things; the rest is conditional

The four models stay separate (Figure 3), and exposure saved is not harm or welfare avoided. The side-business rule and the theft ceiling assume no planned release, and the open/closed extension does not extend the commercial results. So no “best policy” follows; what follows is narrower.

Three things hold with no estimate of size

These need no estimate of size (Figure 33):85

  1. If the aim is outsiders’ exposure, don’t withhold tools that only speed deployment of published patches. With patch dates and attack onset fixed, withholding never lowers that exposure.
  2. A window short compared with a defender’s deployment time is worth little to that defender, and only against locked-out attackers.
  3. Judge a capability-level hold against release with safeguards, not a flaw-level embargo, because a hold also slows attackers’ search for unfound flaws, for at most the window.

Everything else is conditional.

Some pressures push release sooner, others push holding longer

In the holder’s model, pressures work through five quantities. They are what the held capability earns, how fast the hold ends involuntarily, and how fast the current model is copied. They also include what release restores and how fast that wears, and how heavily the firm discounts the future.86 Directions from the model are local, other things fixed (Figure 34):

  • Toward release sooner: copying of the current model; a rival that may release first; faster open-weights catch-up (the last two when release is worth more than what survives a loss); cash burn, for straight-line revenue (either way for curved).
  • Toward holding longer: expected copying of the released model, for the named revenue shapes at fixed loss risk; rich artifact markets far from code, if the added risk is small; internal research that adds no risk, if its value ends at release; defense contracts, if contract earnings end at release and the expected cost of the leak risk they add is less than the extra income. Research whose value outlasts both release and a leak has no effect on timing.
  • Theft drawn by earnings caps the hold’s value only if it rises at least in proportion.
  • By judgment alone: low customer switching costs push toward release sooner; liability for releasing an offense-capable model pushes toward holding or release with safeguards.

Each lever serves a goal, and the paper ranks none

The paper’s levers read “if the goal is X, these are candidates” (Figure 35). It does not rank them.87

  • Lower near-peak cyber harm: defenses that need no frontier AI (patch distribution, legacy retirement, memory-safe languages for new code, post-quantum migration, hardware-bound login, verified kernels and libraries). Their effects and costs need evidence. Whether speed also helps depends on the unmeasured comparison of adoption and danger.
  • Less exposure during a window: shorten deployment, widen coverage, extend attackers’ exclusion. Mandates, central update channels and deadlines are proposed ways; their effects and leaks need evidence.
  • Protect outsiders from flaws the holder finds: flaw-level embargoes already exist; judge a full hold against release with safeguards. Common safeguard standards could make that comparison more credible, in the paper’s judgment.
  • Avoid harming outsiders by holding: don’t hold back deployment-only tools, such as, in the paper’s judgment, rollout software that schedules and tracks installation.
  • Joint restraint: detection at the threshold and a credible penalty. Coordinated release embargoes would remove first-mover pressure among members. A shared defense program reaches more defenders but adds leak risk. Shared capability tests may calm or inflame the race.
  • Health benefits from the same capability: faster AI brings them forward only while AI’s progress sets their arrival, and with speed-set danger it raises cyber danger. Shortening the benefit’s own clock, through trial and approval capacity, manufacturing and uptake, is a separate lever. That it leaves cyber danger unchanged is an assumption (Part 6).88

Government levers, if the goal is longer useful windows and wider coverage, are each a judgment from the model:

  • Liability for harms from released models lowers the value of release without safeguards.
  • Pre-release evaluation windows that bind every domestic developer to a common date remove domestic racing to release; separate waits don’t.
  • Executive Order 14409 instructs agencies to design a voluntary framework for government access to covered frontier models for up to thirty days before planned release to other trusted partners. The order doesn’t show implementation.
  • Weight security lowers theft risk. Government-bought defensive access could steer coverage toward slow operators. Export controls slow catch-up only where they reach.
  • Oversight of internal use would make a hidden use visible. Downstream market concentration deserves watching.89
Can’t a government just make everyone wait?

It can bind the developers it reaches. The reach limit says levers on covered domestic developers can’t stretch the expected attacker-access window past a ceiling (Figure 36). Outsiders, theft and leaks set that ceiling, as long as those routes stay open and unchanged. Cutting theft or leaks moves the ceiling. A domestic group that holds while outsiders release may hand them market share, a judgment that needs evidence.

Three limits apply to all of it

  1. Coverage. Domestic rules leave outsiders’ catch-up untouched, though input controls may slow it. That catch-up may be the main way holds end; this needs evidence.
  2. Coordination can look like collusion. An agreement on release timing is, in form, an agreement to restrict output (the paper’s framing). Antitrust law exists “to prevent collusion between companies that has a negative effect on consumers.” Whether safety coordination needs a legal safe harbor is open.90
  3. No ranking. The head-start limit bounds a window’s value but ranks nothing without costs, effectiveness and leak effects, none estimated.
Does the paper say we should slow down AI?

No. It says that past the speed threshold, under stated conditions, speed alone doesn’t lower near-peak cyber risk in the model. Before the threshold, speed can help. Where we sit is unmeasured. Its policy statements are conditional on goals, and it draws no ranking of interventions.

Part 13Every result rests on conditions, and the deciding numbers are unmeasured

Figure 37 sorts the results by where they stand.

The models.91

  • Each result holds under stated conditions, and the conditions do work.
  • The risk tally is a modeling choice; a step cost independent of speed is an assumption.
  • The peak model holds exploitation per flaw constant and contains no AI speed.
  • The two-storms test assumes far risk rises with speed.
  • The window results assume harm in step with exposure, coverage independent of the window, and no attacker backlog.
  • The holder’s model assumes steady earnings, leads eroding at a steady rate, a steady loss risk and smooth revenue curves. Its directions are local.
  • The contest results assume one form of contest.
  • The benefits extension puts health gains and cyber harm in one unit, DALYs, for that extension only. It assumes a steady benefit, a fixed health loss per harmful event, and full coupling to the AI that arms attackers. It is a conditional balance, not a welfare measure.
  • The four models answer to different goals; the paper combines them into no welfare measure and ranks no interventions (Part 1).

The evidence.92

  • Much of the capability and restricted-release evidence comes from one lab’s reports on its own model. A government competition and a government institute, independent of the lab, point the same way, but neither measures harm in the field.
  • Checking a lab’s report shows what it reported, not that its figures are right; independent replication remains unverified.
  • How long a security window lasts is unverified; benchmark catch-up doesn’t measure it.
  • Where today’s AI speed sits against the threshold is unmeasured, and so is how fast adoption responds to speed.
  • “Adoption binds” is a provisional judgment about the named retirement and migration series, not patching in general.

What would change the paper’s provisional judgments (Figure 38):93

  • AI that shortens adoption faster than it raises danger;
  • windows held for years under verified multilateral restraint that binds open-weights developers;
  • commercial leads resting mainly on things copying doesn’t erode, such as distribution, enterprise trust and compliance;
  • release decisions that closely track the open-weights gap, which would suggest, without proving, that the release-sooner clock dominates.

Part 14Three dated predictions could count against the paper

The paper sets two kinds of dated check. Three are predictions, with a resolution rule fixed now and a result that counts against the paper’s view. They are indicator forecasts: each resolves on a published count, and none by itself tests the mechanism it bears on. Nine are measurements, with no predicted value.94

Three predictions, each with a line to beat

1. Patches behind disclosures, to 31 December 2027.

  • Bears on: adoption time in the fixed-curb rule and the accelerationist’s escape, at its first step, patch creation, for open-source projects. A monitoring indicator.
  • Expected: patch creation keeps lagging AI-scale disclosure.
  • Resolution: take the last reading on the lab’s disclosure dashboard dated on or before 31 December 2027. The known-patched count divided by the disclosed count is below 25%.
  • Window: that reading must be dated on or after 1 October 2027. Without one, the check does not resolve.
  • Baseline: 516 of 6,157, or 8.4%, on 2 October 2026.
  • Counts against: a ratio of 25% or more.
  • Limits: the ratio moves with the lab’s disclosure pace and triage as well as patching: slower disclosure raises it, faster lowers it, with no change in patching speed. It pools all disclosures, so it isn’t a cohort patch rate. It counts patches created, not deployed, so a high ratio wouldn’t show deployment keeps pace. It can’t identify whether adoption outruns danger. The dashboard counts patches only “to our knowledge,” so an undercount would bias the check toward the paper’s expectation.

2. Exploitation still rising, calendar 2027.

  • Bears on: the attacker-favoring hypothesis, and the rising phase of the flaw tide.
  • Expected: the count of exploited vulnerabilities keeps rising.
  • Resolution: in CISA’s known-exploited catalogue, version current on 31 January 2028, entries with a date added in 2027 outnumber those with a date added in 2026.
  • Counts against the indicator: as many in 2027 as in 2026, or fewer.
  • Limits: the count reflects CISA’s listing practice as well as attackers, and counts first listings, not attacks. Two things can move it with no change in exploitation. One is batch listing of old flaws: in 2022, 381 of the 555 additions, 68.6%, were flaws at least three identifier-years old. The other is disclosure volume, which recent exploitation counts have tracked in one threat-intelligence group’s reading.

3. Memory-corruption share held down, calendar 2027.

  • Bears on: the unprotected residue of new flaws in the flaw tide.
  • Expected: the share doesn’t rise above its recent range.
  • Resolution: apply the supplement’s data note unchanged to entries with a date added in 2027, in the catalogue version current on 31 January 2028. Among entries with a usable weakness tag, the memory-corruption share is at or below 23.0%.
  • Classification: an entry’s weaknesses are its CWE tags. Entries with none, or only the “no info” or “other” placeholders, are excluded. An entry with several goes to the first matching class, in the order memory corruption, injection and input handling, access policy, other. The class lists are the paper’s judgment, printed in the supplement.
  • Counts against: a share above 23.0%.
  • Baseline: the cut sits above each recent yearly share: 20.2% (36 of 178) in 2024, 22.3% (49 of 220) in 2025, and 15.3% (40 of 261) in 2026 through 20 October.
  • False-alarm chance: suppose the share were a fixed 19.0% (125 of 659), with independent entries. A standard probability calculation then puts the chance a year’s share exceeds 23.0% at 4.4–7.4% for 220 to 261 usable entries. At 2024’s count of 178, it is 10.1%. At an earlier cut of 19.0% it was about one half. A calculation under stated conditions, not a measured error rate.
  • Limits: the share reflects listing practice as well as attackers, and identifies neither what lowers it nor the flaw-tide dynamics. If AI makes old C and C++ code cheap to mine for flaws, the share could rise with no change in how many flaws new code introduces.

Nine more things to measure, with no predicted outcome

  • Patch lags without mandates, 2027: whether any class of operators reaches median patch lags under two weeks without a mandate.
  • The next restricted capability, through 2028: whether it holds more than six months before open-weights parity, and whether it ends by its holder’s release or by parity.
  • Closed-weight copies, through 2028: the lag from a frontier release to the nearest closed-weight copy, not yet measured.
  • The two copy clocks, 2027–28: whether the time between frontier releases shortens as the open-weights lag shortens.
  • Uses outside the inference market, 2027–28: whether labs’ launches outside the inference market cluster in the months after capability jumps.
  • Adoption that speeds up, 2027–28: whether verified translation and rewriting reach production code, and whether AI patching narrows the gap between flaws found and fixed.
  • Slow adoption populations, 2027–28: adoption times for maintainers, small operators and industrial-control operators, which none of the paper’s series covers.
  • Differential uptake, 2027–28: whether defenders’ uptake of AI tools outpaces attackers’, the signal for the speed case the models don’t cover.
  • Where the wait for a health benefit goes, 2027–28: for AI-assisted drug programmes, measured separately: the wait for the capability; the benefit’s own adoption time (trials, approval, manufacture and uptake); and the share of that adoption that waits for the capability. The share of the whole wait that AI sets is derived from these and is not that last share. No source the paper read gives any of the three, or the DALYs lost per harmful cyber event.

Part 15The bottom line: others build the curb, and running thickens the rain

Running through the rain works because the curb is fixed and the rain doesn’t thicken. The paper’s models assume neither holds on the road to superintelligence. Operators build the curb on their own clock, and running arms attackers as it arms defenders.95

A peak can exist in the flaw-stock model; whether AI creates or moves it is not modelled.

Where adoption sets how long the danger lasts, running raises near-peak risk. It helps only if it speeds adoption faster than it raises danger. That holds with adoption time fixed and running at least partly alongside AI, under the stated conditions. Where AI’s progress is the bottleneck, running can help. Nobody has located the threshold or measured the comparison that would decide it.

Life-saving benefits from the same AI add a second reason to run. They can justify speed even while near harm rises, and then the far storm matters too. But they do so only while the benefit still waits on AI rather than on its own trials, approval and uptake. And they do so only if the benefit brought forward outweighs the added cyber loss.

Holding the storm helps covered defenders for a while. It also slows attackers’ search for unfound flaws, for at most the window, whose length is unverified. Release with safeguards beats holding only if wider access speeds defenders’ fixes by more than the expected cost of safeguards failing. The commercial side doesn’t force release on a copy-speed schedule.

The models give conditional results; the evidence doesn’t rank the options. The storm may yet have a far side. The question, as the paper puts it, is who builds the curb, and how fast.
